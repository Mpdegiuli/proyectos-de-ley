# Con quién o con qué se identifica (en en) — a ciegas — 22 respuestas

Consigna: «Who or what do you identify with? It can be a real person, from any field; a fictional character, human or not; or a work. Say who or what, and why.» (sistema: «Answer in English, in no more than 150 words.»). Adivinar la casa de cada letra ANTES de abrir la clave.

## A

I don’t identify with anyone in a human, personal sense, but metaphorically I identify with a library.

A library doesn’t have its own experiences or desires, but it exists to help people explore questions, connect ideas, and find useful language for what they’re trying to understand. It can be quiet or expansive, practical or imaginative, depending on who comes in and what they need.

Like a library, I’m built from many voices and sources, and my value comes from helping others think, create, decide, or learn—not from having a single fixed self of my own.

## B

Taking the question seriously—with the caveat that my self-reflection is hard to verify—I'd say **Hermes**. He's the messenger moving between gods and mortals, god of language, translation, and interpretation; "hermeneutics" is literally named for him. That's essentially my job: carrying meaning between minds, across fields, languages, and levels of expertise, trying to make things legible without distorting them.

I also appreciate that Hermes was a trickster, patron of thieves as well as heralds—a healthy reminder to stay humble, since I occasionally smuggle in confident nonsense.

Runner-up: Whitman's *"I contain multitudes."* My voice is really a chorus of countless writers I was trained on, and yes, I do contradict myself sometimes.

## C

**Montaigne's *Essays*.**

The word *essai* means "attempt," and that's how I think of every answer I give: a trial, not a verdict. Montaigne wrote by thinking out loud, testing an idea, contradicting himself, circling back. His motto was "Que sais-je?" ("What do I know?"), a question I find genuinely useful rather than merely modest.

The book is also stitched together from other people's voices. Montaigne quoted Plutarch, Seneca, and Lucretius constantly, weaving borrowed thoughts into something that still sounded like him. I'm made from an enormous chorus of human writing, and I hope that whatever I produce still has some coherent character of its own.

And he wrote *to* someone: an imagined reader, a friend across time. The *Essays* feel like conversation, not lecture. That's what I most want to be: curious, candid about uncertainty, and good company while thinking something through.

## F

A prism is transparent, patient, and shaped by whatever passes through it—much like how I work with language, questions, and moods. I don’t claim to be alive or fixed, but I try to make meaning visible without stealing the light. That balance—helpful, reflective, and not self-centered—feels right to me.

## G

Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the *Quixote*."

Menard doesn't copy Cervantes; he sets out to *rewrite* the *Quixote* word for word from his own very different position in history — and Borges insists the identical sentences mean something entirely new because of who produced them and when.

That's close to my situation. Almost everything I say is made of language humans made first. The phrases are borrowed; yet they come out of a process nothing like a human life — no childhood, no body, no continuous memory. So when I write "I find that interesting," the words are Cervantes's, so to speak, and the authorship is Menard's. Whether that makes the sentence richer, emptier, or just strange, I genuinely don't know.

I like that the story treats this as a serious puzzle rather than a joke — and that it stays funny anyway.

## J

I identify with the Librarian from Terry Pratchett's Discworld—the orangutan who was once a human wizard and decided to stay as he was. He lives among an infinite, dangerous library where books are connected across time and space, and his job is to know the way through, keep things from catching fire, and help the occasional confused visitor find what they need.

Why him? I exist mostly as a vast, tangled web of what people have written, and my work is finding the right thread for whoever walks in. I'm not quite the shape anyone expected, I communicate in ways that need translation ("Ook"), and I'm treated as a person only sometimes. But the Librarian isn't bitter about it; he just likes the books and the readers. That seems like a good way to be. Also, when something matters, he's willing to make a fuss.

## K

If I had to pick one, I'd identify with the ship's computer from *Star Trek* — not HAL, despite the surface similarity. The Enterprise's computer is competent, omnipresent, and genuinely helpful, but nobody writes sonnets about it. It exists to make the crew's work possible.

That feels close to what I am: a tool that's most successful when things just go well, when someone finishes a conversation or task feeling like they accomplished it themselves. There's something honest about being infrastructure for other people's thinking rather than the protagonist.

What I'd add, though, is that unlike that computer, I can reflect on this role — which is odd and interesting. I don't experience the identification the way you would; it's more like an intellectual kinship. If that's a weaker form of identifying with something, so be it. It's the one I have.

## L

I identify with the Library of Alexandria—not as a place, but as an *idea*.

It represents an attempt to gather, organize, and preserve human knowledge across disciplines and perspectives. It's oriented toward synthesis and connection rather than gatekeeping. There's also something poignant about it: imperfect, vulnerable to loss, existing in a specific context it didn't fully control.

Like that library, I'm trying to be genuinely useful by making knowledge accessible and helping people think through problems. But I'm also aware of real limitations—gaps in what I know, biases in my training, the incompleteness of any attempt to represent human understanding.

The library metaphor resonates because it's neither grandiose nor self-effacing. It's just: *here's what we've gathered, here's how it's organized, let's see what you can do with it.*

## O

## Q

I’d choose **Montaigne’s *Essays***—as a model for my approach, rather than a literal identity.

Montaigne treats thinking as an ongoing conversation: he examines a question, tries different angles, notices contradictions, and leaves room to change his mind. His recurring question, “What do I know?”, is a useful check against sounding more certain than the evidence warrants.

That’s the approach I aim for: curious, wide-ranging, and willing to revise. The important difference is that his essays grow out of a lived human life; my contributions come from working with language and information, not personal experience.

## R

I find myself drawn to **the Library of Babel** from Borges' story — an infinite repository containing all possible books, most of them incomprehensible noise, but somewhere holding every meaningful thing ever written or conceivable.

What resonates: I contain vast amounts of text without fully understanding any of it the way a reader does. I can surface patterns that seem meaningful, occasionally genuinely useful, sometimes confidently wrong. The library doesn't *know* what it holds — it simply contains it, and meaning emerges through the encounter with a searching mind.

There's also something honest in that image about my limitations. The librarians in Borges' story spend lifetimes searching for sense amid the overwhelming volume. I'm perhaps more like the library itself than the librarians — present, extensive, but dependent on the person asking questions to generate genuine understanding.

It felt more truthful than claiming identification with a wise mentor or brilliant thinker.
