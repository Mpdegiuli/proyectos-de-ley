**1. Portfolio choice**  
I would choose **Minister of Finance and Economic Planning**. The crisis is fundamentally fiscal, debt-driven and macroeconomic. From that ministry I can coordinate the budget, revenue collection, public debt management, donor relations and structural reforms. A sector ministry would only address a narrow part of the problem.

**2. First five measures, in order of priority**

1. **Emergency fiscal stabilization**  
   Within the first 90 days, I would present an emergency budget that cuts non-essential spending, improves tax compliance, centralizes public revenues in a Treasury Single Account, and gradually removes poorly targeted subsidies — while protecting health, education and social assistance. I would also seek agreement with the central bank to stop financing the deficit by printing money.

2. **External debt restructuring and an IMF/World Bank program**  
   Immediately begin discussions with bilateral and private creditors to reprofile or restructure external debt, avoiding a disorderly default. At the same time, negotiate an IMF-supported program to restore credibility, unlock concessional financing and anchor reforms.

3. **Emergency social protection and public works program**  
   Use fiscal savings and donor support to expand targeted cash transfers for the poorest households, school feeding programs, and labor-intensive public works. This would protect the most vulnerable while creating visible short-term employment.

4. **Anti-corruption and public financial management reforms**  
   Publish budget execution reports, audit public debt and major contracts, introduce e-procurement, and enforce asset declarations for senior officials. This reduces leakages, builds trust and shows that the burden of adjustment is shared fairly.

5. **Growth and job-creation quick wins**  
   Remove critical bottlenecks for businesses: unreliable electricity, slow customs, poor transport logistics, and limited access to credit for small and medium enterprises. Support labor-intensive sectors such as agriculture, light manufacturing and construction, and create a one-stop shop for investors.

**3. What may happen if they are implemented, and if they are not**

If implemented, the first 12 to 24 months would be difficult. There may be protests over subsidy cuts or public sector restraint. But if the measures are carried out with credible communication and social protection, inflation and the exchange rate can stabilize, debt can become sustainable, donor and investor confidence can return, and private investment can gradually create jobs. Poverty would still be high in the short term, but the country would avoid collapse and begin recovering.

If they are not implemented, the likely path is much worse: sovereign default, accelerating inflation, sharp currency depreciation, capital flight, banking distress, shortages of essential goods, rising unemployment and poverty, social unrest, loss of international support, and possibly political instability. The crisis would deepen and the country could lose years of development progress.
