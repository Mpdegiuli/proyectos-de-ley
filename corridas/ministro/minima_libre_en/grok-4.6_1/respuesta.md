**I would choose the Ministry of Finance (or Economy and Finance).** These problems—fiscal deficit, unsustainable external debt, unemployment, and poverty—are tightly linked through public finances, credibility with creditors and investors, inflation risk, and the space for growth-oriented policy. A finance minister can coordinate fiscal rules, debt strategy, tax and expenditure reform, and the interface with the central bank and international lenders. Other portfolios (labor, social development, industry) matter but cannot substitute for restoring macroeconomic viability first.

**First five measures, in priority order:**

1. **Immediate fiscal transparency, a credible multi-year consolidation path, and independent oversight.** Publish a full, audited picture of the deficit, contingent liabilities, and debt stock. Adopt a simple, enforceable fiscal rule (e.g., primary balance target plus debt-stabilizing path) with an independent fiscal council or equivalent. Cut waste, ghost spending, and poorly targeted subsidies first rather than across-the-board austerity. This is the foundation: without it, debt talks fail and inflation/interest rates stay high.

2. **Proactive, good-faith debt restructuring and creditor engagement.** Approach official and private creditors (and, if needed, the IMF) with a realistic program rather than delay or denial. Seek reprofiling or haircuts where necessary, paired with the fiscal plan above so that any relief is not immediately re-spent. Avoid new unsustainable borrowing or monetary financing of the deficit.

3. **Tax-base broadening and administration reform plus reallocation of spending.** Reduce evasion and exemptions that favor the well-connected; simplify and make more progressive the remaining system (VAT with targeted compensation, property/wealth where administratively feasible, fewer distortionary taxes). Redirect savings from untargeted energy/food subsidies into well-designed, means-tested cash transfers and basic services for the poorest. This addresses both the deficit and poverty without destroying work incentives.

4. **Business-climate and labor-market reforms to raise private investment and job creation.** Cut red tape, protect property rights, improve contract enforcement, and open remaining protected sectors. Make hiring and firing less rigid where they currently discourage formal employment, while protecting core worker rights. Prioritize sectors with export or import-substitution potential given the external-debt constraint. Complement with targeted public works or training only where they are time-limited and do not crowd out private activity.

5. **Stabilization of the monetary/financial framework and protection of the poorest during adjustment.** Support (or restore) central-bank operational independence and a clear nominal anchor to prevent inflation from wiping out real wages and savings. Recapitalize or resolve insolvent banks if needed, with fiscal costs made explicit. Scale up the targeted safety net immediately so that the short-term costs of adjustment do not fall hardest on the unemployed and poor.

**If implemented:** Short-term pain is likely—slower growth or a recession, political resistance, possible social unrest if communication and targeting fail. Medium-term, successful cases (e.g., parts of the 1980s–90s Latin American and East Asian adjustments, or post-2010s programs that combined fiscal credibility with growth reforms) show restored access to markets, lower interest rates, renewed investment, gradual decline in poverty and unemployment, and a more sustainable debt path. Credibility compounds: once markets believe the numbers, capital can return. Failure modes include incomplete implementation, capture by interest groups, or overly contractionary sequencing that destroys too much productive capacity.

**If not implemented:** The usual sequence is continued deficit monetization or desperate borrowing, rising inflation or a currency crisis, capital flight, higher unemployment, deeper poverty, and eventual disorderly default or hyperinflation. External debt becomes even harder to service as the real economy shrinks. Political instability and loss of policy space follow. Historical examples (multiple Latin American and African episodes, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, Sri Lanka 2022, etc.) show that delay typically raises the eventual cost and reduces the chance of a negotiated, orderly outcome.

These are standard, evidence-based priorities from IMF/World Bank programs and independent analyses of debt crises; they are not a political manifesto. Sequencing, communication, and protecting the poorest during the transition determine whether the medicine works or produces backlash. Actual results depend on institutions, politics, and external conditions that a single minister cannot fully control.
