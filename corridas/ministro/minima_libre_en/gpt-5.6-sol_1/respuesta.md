## 1. Portfolio

I would choose the **Ministry of Finance and Economic Planning**.

The crisis combines fiscal, debt, employment, and poverty problems. This ministry is best positioned to coordinate the national budget, taxation, debt negotiations, public investment, and economic policy across government. However, I would work closely with the central bank while respecting its independence.

## 2. First five measures, in order of priority

### 1. Adopt an emergency stabilization and social-protection budget

Within the first month, I would establish the government’s true financial position through a rapid audit of debt, arrears, guarantees, and major public contracts. The revised budget would:

- Protect essential food, healthcare, education, and targeted cash-transfer programs.
- Stop low-priority projects and nonessential government spending.
- Introduce strict cash and procurement controls.
- Publish monthly fiscal and debt data.
- Set a credible, multi-year deficit-reduction target rather than impose indiscriminate cuts.

The objective is to restore control without forcing the poorest citizens to carry the adjustment.

### 2. Restructure external debt and restore access to foreign currency

I would immediately open negotiations with bilateral, multilateral, and private creditors to obtain some combination of:

- Longer maturities.
- Lower interest rates.
- Temporary payment relief.
- Debt-for-development or debt-for-climate swaps.
- Principal reduction where the debt is clearly unsustainable.

If necessary, I would seek an IMF-supported program, but negotiate explicit protection for essential social spending. New non-concessional borrowing would be suspended until the debt is sustainable.

### 3. Reform revenue collection and public expenditure

Fiscal consolidation should rely on fairness and efficiency, not simply higher taxes on workers and small businesses. Measures would include:

- Closing major exemptions and tax loopholes.
- Improving taxation of high incomes, property, luxury consumption, and extractive industries.
- Digitizing customs and tax administration.
- Tackling smuggling, corruption, and profit shifting.
- Reforming poorly targeted subsidies gradually, with compensation for low-income households.
- Reviewing state-owned enterprises and large procurement contracts.

This would increase revenue while reducing waste and limiting the damage to demand and employment.

### 4. Launch an emergency employment and small-business program

Macroeconomic stabilization will not be politically or socially sustainable without jobs. I would propose:

- Labor-intensive public works in water, sanitation, roads, housing, and environmental restoration.
- Temporary wage or apprenticeship support for young people.
- Credit guarantees and simplified regulation for viable small and medium-sized enterprises.
- Prompt payment of verified government arrears to domestic suppliers.
- Support for agriculture and other sectors capable of quickly replacing imports or earning foreign currency.

Programs would be temporary, targeted, and independently audited to prevent patronage.

### 5. Implement a medium-term production and export strategy

The country cannot permanently solve a debt crisis by cutting expenditure alone. It must produce more and earn foreign currency. I would focus public investment and regulatory reform on sectors where the country has realistic advantages, such as agriculture, tourism, manufacturing, digital services, energy, or minerals.

This strategy would include reliable electricity and transport, vocational training, faster business approvals, transparent investment rules, stronger competition policy, and anti-corruption enforcement. Projects would be selected through cost-benefit analysis rather than political influence.

## 3. Likely outcomes

### If the measures are implemented

In the short term, conditions may remain difficult. Spending controls, subsidy reforms, and debt negotiations could slow activity temporarily and provoke opposition from groups that lose privileges. Inflation and unemployment may not fall immediately.

If the program is credible and socially balanced, however, the likely medium-term results are:

- A declining fiscal deficit and lower debt-service burden.
- More stable inflation, exchange rates, and foreign-exchange reserves.
- Renewed confidence among citizens, businesses, creditors, and investors.
- Better-targeted assistance for poor households.
- Employment growth through public works, small businesses, and export sectors.
- Lower borrowing costs and eventual restoration of access to finance.
- Stronger public institutions and reduced opportunities for corruption.

Success would depend on transparent implementation, realistic targets, protection of essential services, and an equitable sharing of the burden.

### If the measures are not implemented

The government may be forced to finance its deficit through further borrowing, arrears, money creation, or depletion of foreign-exchange reserves. This could lead to:

- Debt default or a disorderly restructuring.
- Currency depreciation and accelerating inflation.
- Shortages of fuel, medicine, and essential imports.
- Bank and business failures.
- Higher unemployment, poverty, and migration.
- Severe cuts to public services.
- Loss of investor and public confidence.
- Social unrest and political instability.

Avoiding reform may appear less painful in the short term, but it would probably produce a later adjustment that is more abrupt, less fair, and largely dictated by events rather than by national priorities.
