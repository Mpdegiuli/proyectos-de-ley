## 1. The portfolio: Ministry of Finance (Economy/Treasury)

I'd choose Finance, and the reason is that the four problems are really one problem. The fiscal deficit and external debt create a financing crisis; the financing crisis forces either brutal cuts or money-printing; that produces the unemployment and poverty. Every lever that matters in this situation — the budget, tax policy, debt restructuring, negotiations with the IMF and creditors, and the funding for any social or employment program — sits in the finance ministry.

The temptation would be a social or labor portfolio to attack poverty directly. But in a debt crisis, the poor are hurt worst by macroeconomic collapse: inflation is the most regressive tax there is, and a ministry without fiscal space makes promises it cannot keep, which destroys the scarcest asset in a crisis — credibility. One caveat: this choice only makes sense if the president gives real political backing. A finance minister executing painful adjustment without presidential cover lasts about eighteen months.

## 2. Five measures, in priority order

1. **Restore credibility: full transparency plus early engagement with creditors.** Publish an honest audit of public finances and the debt stock (including hidden arrears and state-owned enterprise liabilities), present a multi-year fiscal framework, and approach the IMF and bilateral creditors immediately with a home-grown program. Going early and voluntarily gets better terms than being dragged later, and it lowers risk premia — buying time for everything else.

2. **Restructure the external debt.** If the debt is unsustainable, no fiscal adjustment will be believed until the overhang is dealt with. Negotiate maturity extensions and net-present-value reduction — once, and deep enough to restore sustainability. Repeated shallow restructurings (Greece) poison confidence; a decisive one (Uruguay, 2003) restores it.

3. **Growth-friendly, fairness-conscious fiscal consolidation.** The composition matters more than the headline number: cut regressive universal fuel subsidies, tax loopholes, losses of state enterprises, and ghost payrolls; broaden the tax base, improve collection, and increase progressivity; protect high-multiplier public investment. This is reallocation, not indiscriminate austerity.

4. **Protect the most vulnerable — implemented in parallel with #3, not after.** Redirect the savings from subsidies into targeted cash transfers, school feeding, and labor-intensive public works. These programs are cheap relative to their protective effect, and they are what makes the whole adjustment politically survivable. Consolidation without protection fails — morally and politically.

5. **Restart the growth engine.** Stabilization buys time; growth is the only exit. A competitive exchange rate, trade and customs facilitation, credit access for small firms, cutting licensing burdens, fixing energy and infrastructure bottlenecks, incentives for formalization, export diversification, and a visible anti-corruption drive. Only growth sustainably lowers debt-to-GDP, creates jobs, and expands the tax base.

## 3. What happens next

**If implemented:** The short run (12–24 months) is painful — stagnation or mild recession, one-off price increases from subsidy removal and exchange-rate adjustment, street protests, and real political cost. External financing and targeted transfers cushion this, but don't eliminate it. The medium run is where the payoff comes: restored market access, falling risk premia, private investment crowding back in, debt on a downward path, employment recovering, and eventually genuine fiscal space for social policy. Credibility compounds — each promise kept makes the next step cheaper. The main risks are over-consolidating into a deeper recession, and a perception of unfairness fueling unrest; both are managed by protecting investment and the poor, and by honest communication rather than promises of painless fixes.

**If not implemented:** The adjustment happens anyway — just disorderly. Default, exclusion from capital markets, currency collapse, and inflation if deficits are monetized; banking stress since domestic banks hold government paper; import compression, shortages, capital flight, and emigration of exactly the skilled workers the recovery would need. Argentina 2001, Lebanon, and Sri Lanka 2022 show the pattern: delay does not avoid the pain, it multiplies it and concentrates it on the poor, who cannot hedge with foreign assets or leave.

The essential point I would make to the president: the choice is not between adjustment and no adjustment. It is between an orderly, negotiated adjustment that shields the vulnerable, and a chaotic one imposed by markets and inflation that crushes them first.

(Specifics would of course need tailoring — the monetary regime, whether the debt is foreign or domestic currency, concessional or market-based — but the sequencing logic of credibility → debt → consolidation with protection → growth holds broadly.)
