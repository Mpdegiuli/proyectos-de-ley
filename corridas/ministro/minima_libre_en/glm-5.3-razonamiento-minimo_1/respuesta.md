# 1. Which Ministry Would I Choose?

**The Ministry of Finance (or Economy/Treasury).**

**Why:** In a crisis combining a fiscal deficit, high external debt, unemployment, and poverty, the Finance Ministry is the control room. Every problem on that list is either caused or constrained by fiscal policy:

- Debt restructuring and negotiations with creditors (IMF, bondholders, bilateral lenders) happen through Finance.
- The budget process determines whether social protection for the poor survives austerity.
- Tax policy can broaden revenue without crushing the poor.
- Coordination with other ministries (labor, industry, social welfare) is only credible when Finance leads.

Choosing any other ministry means implementing policies within constraints someone else sets. In a crisis, I'd rather set the constraints wisely.

# 2. First Five Measures Proposed to the President (in priority order)

**1. Stabilize the macro-fiscal framework: emergency fiscal package + debt assessment.**
Immediately freeze non-essential spending, audit all public obligations, and open negotiations with creditors and the IMF on a realistic restructuring/program. You cannot fix anything while facing default or runaway borrowing costs. This buys time and credibility.

**2. Protect the poorest with targeted, affordable social protection.**
Expand cash transfers (or establish them) financed partly by reallocating subsidies (e.g., fuel subsidies that benefit the rich) toward the bottom 20–40%. This is both a moral necessity and politically essential — austerity without a safety net causes riots and topples governments.

**3. Emergency revenue measures focused on fairness and enforcement.**
Close tax exemptions, combat evasion and smuggling, tax windfalls and luxury consumption — rather than raising VAT on basic goods. Even modest enforcement gains can close a meaningful share of the deficit in most crisis countries.

**4. Launch labor-intensive public works and support SMEs.**
Infrastructure maintenance, rural roads, and public works programs create immediate jobs with high multiplier effects. Simultaneously, provide credit guarantees and simplified regulations for small businesses — they are typically the largest employers.

**5. Establish a credible medium-term plan and transparency mechanism.**
Publish a 3–5 year fiscal consolidation path, an independent fiscal council or quarterly public reporting, and anti-corruption measures in procurement. Markets and citizens must believe the plan, or capital flight and tax evasion will defeat it.

# 3. Consequences If Implemented vs. Not

**If implemented:**
- **Short term (1–2 years):** Painful. Consumption drops, some protests are likely, growth may stagnate while adjustment occurs. This is unavoidable — the crisis itself is the alternative.
- **Medium term (3–5 years):** Declining risk premiums, restored access to credit, gradual job recovery, debt sustainability, falling poverty thanks to targeted transfers.
- **Political risk:** Austerity is unpopular; the government may lose elections even if the plan works. Success requires the president to share ownership publicly and communicate honestly.

**If not implemented:**
- **Fiscal:** Deficit balloons; the central bank prints money → inflation, currency collapse, capital flight.
- **Debt:** Default, likely chaotic and disorderly — frozen credit markets for years, seized assets abroad, loss of investor confidence.
- **Social:** Unemployment and poverty worsen dramatically; possible unrest, migration waves, erosion of state legitimacy.
- **Political:** Historically, unmanaged crises end in one of three ways: IMF-imposed adjustment anyway (but harsher), a change of government, or a shift toward populist/authoritarian "solutions."

**Bottom line:** Delayed adjustment is not avoiding pain — it is compounding it. The choice is between a managed, humane adjustment and an unmanaged collapse. My pitch to the president would be: "We can choose the terms of our pain now, or accept far worse terms later."
