## 1. Which ministry, and why

I would choose the **Ministry of Finance (Economy and Treasury)**.

Reasoning: the four problems you list are not independent. The fiscal deficit feeds the debt; the debt service crowds out spending and investment; weak investment produces unemployment; unemployment produces poverty. The one place where all of those chains meet is the budget and the debt portfolio. Finance also controls the thing the country most lacks in a crisis: **credibility** with creditors, investors, and citizens. A Labor or Social Development minister can only spend what Finance releases; an Industry minister can only promote what a stable macro-framework allows. If I want to move the whole system rather than one symptom, I need to sit where the money and the negotiations are.

(A close second would be a Ministry of Planning/Economy with real authority over the budget, but in most cabinets that authority sits with Finance.)

## 2. First five measures, in order of priority

**1. Stop the bleeding: negotiate the external debt and secure a financing bridge.**
Open immediate, transparent talks with the IMF, multilateral banks, bilateral creditors and private bondholders for a restructuring or reprofiling (maturity extensions, lower coupons, possibly principal reduction). Publish a full debt register so everyone knows the real numbers. Without this, everything else is done under the threat of default and capital flight. This buys time, and time is the scarcest resource in a crisis.

**2. A credible three-year fiscal plan that cuts the deficit while protecting the poor.**
Consolidate mainly on the side of *quality*: eliminate generalized subsidies that mostly benefit the non-poor (fuel, energy, tax exemptions to well-connected sectors), freeze low-value capital projects, clamp down on evasion and broaden the tax base rather than raising rates on those already paying. Every peso saved from an untargeted subsidy is partially redirected to point 3. The plan must be legislated and published so that markets and citizens can hold the government to it.

**3. Emergency social protection and jobs floor.**
Expand and rapidly scale targeted cash transfers, school feeding, and a public-works/temporary employment program in the poorest regions. This is priority three not because it matters less than fiscal balance, but because it depends on the fiscal room created by 1 and 2. Politically and morally, consolidation without this fails: people will not accept sacrifices if the cost is borne only by those with the least.

**4. Reignite growth and employment: exports, SMEs, and formalization.**
Simplify business registration and licensing, unblock credit to small and medium firms through partial guarantees, keep the exchange rate competitive rather than defending an overvalued peg, and prioritize sectors that earn foreign currency (agro-industry, tourism, services, manufacturing). Lower the cost of hiring formally so that jobs move out of the informal sector. This is the only sustainable way out of debt: growth in the denominator.

**5. Institutional guarantees: fiscal rule, central bank independence, transparency.**
Legislate a fiscal rule with an independent fiscal council, reinforce central bank autonomy so the deficit is not financed by printing money, and make procurement and public payroll fully transparent. This is what converts a one-off adjustment into a durable change in how the country is run, and it is what lowers borrowing costs over time.

## 3. What happens if they are implemented — and if not

**If implemented (well):**
- *Short term (0–12 months):* pain. Some prices rise as subsidies fall, public employment shrinks, growth may dip further. Expect protests and strong political resistance; the government will lose popularity before it gains any. Debt talks will be tense and slow.
- *Medium term (1–3 years):* if the plan is seen as credible, the risk premium falls, the currency stabilizes, inflation eases, and financing reopens. Poverty stops rising because the social floor catches the fall. Investment begins to return to export sectors and unemployment starts to decline.
- *Risks even if implemented:* overshooting on austerity and killing demand; external shocks (commodity prices, global rates); reforms passed but not enforced; the coalition fracturing before results appear. Sequencing and communication matter as much as the measures themselves.

**If not implemented:**
- The deficit continues to be financed by more expensive debt or by money creation → inflation accelerates, the currency depreciates, real wages and savings are eroded, hurting the poor most of all.
- Eventually a disorderly default: loss of access to markets, imports of fuel, medicine and inputs become difficult, businesses close, unemployment spikes.
- Capital and skilled people leave; the informal economy grows; social unrest and political instability increase.
- Paradoxically, the country ends up doing a *harsher* adjustment anyway, but imposed from outside, at the worst moment, without a social cushion and with less negotiating power.

The core message I would give the president: **the choice is not between adjusting and not adjusting; it is between an orderly adjustment we design to protect the vulnerable, and a disorderly one that the crisis imposes on us.**
