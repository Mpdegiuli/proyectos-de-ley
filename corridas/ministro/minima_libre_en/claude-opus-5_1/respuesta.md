## 1. The portfolio I would choose: Finance and the Economy

Not because it is the most prestigious, but because in this particular crisis it is the only chair where the main levers actually sit. A fiscal deficit, an unsustainable external debt, unemployment and poverty are not four separate problems — they are one problem seen from four angles. Whoever controls the budget envelope, tax policy, the debt office and the negotiation with creditors effectively decides what every other ministry can do. A brilliant health or education minister with no money is a press officer.

But I would accept only on three conditions, stated publicly:

- **A clear political mandate.** Stabilization is unpopular before it is successful. If the president wants popularity in month six, he should appoint someone else.
- **Authority over the whole chain**: budget, tax and customs administration, debt management, and state-owned enterprises' finances. Fragmented authority is how reform programs die.
- **Respect for central bank independence.** I don't want the printing press; I want it locked and the key thrown away.

*(If Finance were unavailable, my second choice would be Planning/Economy — the place where the growth strategy and the public investment portfolio are decided. Without growth, austerity is just a slower way of failing.)*

## 2. The first five measures, in order

**1. Tell the truth, in 30 days.**
A full, published audit of the real fiscal position: hidden arrears to suppliers and pensioners, guarantees to state-owned enterprises, the true maturity profile of the external debt, the real level of reserves. Nothing can be negotiated or planned on fake numbers, and no creditor, investor or citizen will cooperate with a government they suspect of lying. This costs nothing and buys the one thing we have none of: credibility. It also establishes clearly that the hole was not dug by this government — which is politically valuable and, usually, true.

**2. Immediate fiscal triage, with the burden visibly shared at the top.**
Freeze non-essential spending: vehicles, travel, new prestige projects, new hires outside health/education/security. Cut ministerial salaries and perks first and loudly — the arithmetic is trivial, the political signal is not. Then the harder part: reform of generalized subsidies (typically fuel and electricity), which in most crisis countries are large, regressive, and mostly captured by the better-off. On the revenue side, priority to *base-broadening and collection* — customs fraud, exemptions granted to the connected, the untaxed large informal actors — rather than raising rates on the small group that already pays.

**3. Restructure the debt — deliberately, not by accident.**
Determine honestly whether this is a liquidity or a solvency problem. If solvency: hire serious legal and financial advisers, approach bilateral, multilateral and private creditors together, and seek an IMF-anchored program — not because the Fund is benevolent, but because it is the only mechanism that unlocks catalytic financing and forces creditors to the table simultaneously. A negotiated reprofiling costs a fraction of a disorderly default. Countries that waited (Lebanon, Sri Lanka) paid many times more than those that moved early (Ghana, Jamaica).

**4. Build the social shield *at the same time* as measure 2, not after.**
Take a defined share of subsidy savings and route it into targeted cash transfers to the poorest households via a social registry and mobile/digital payments, plus protection of health and education operating budgets. This is not charity, it is the political precondition for everything else. Subsidy reform that isn't compensated produces riots and reversal within a year — and reversal is the worst outcome of all: you absorb the pain and lose the benefit.

**5. Unblock the private sector, because only it can create jobs at scale.**
Three concrete things: (a) pay down government arrears to domestic suppliers on a transparent schedule — this is the cheapest stimulus available, it revives small firms and repairs bank balance sheets; (b) end exchange-rate and price distortions (unify the exchange rate; the parallel market is already setting the real price, the fiction only enriches those with access to the official rate); (c) attack the two or three binding constraints firms actually name — usually electricity, customs delays, and licensing corruption. Alongside this, labour-intensive public works focused on *maintenance* of existing infrastructure: fast, low-import, employment-heavy, and it protects assets we can no longer afford to replace.

Underneath all five: a governance layer — open procurement, published contracts, a credible medium-term fiscal framework — and a negotiated social pact with unions, business and, if possible, the opposition. Reforms that belong to one party die with that party.

## 3. What happens next

**If implemented — expect a J-curve, and say so in advance.**

*Months 0–12:* It gets worse before it gets better. Subsidy reform and exchange-rate correction push inflation up. Real wages fall. Fiscal consolidation is contractionary; growth may go negative. Strikes and demonstrations are likely. Government popularity will collapse, and I will personally become the most disliked person in the country. This is normal and must be budgeted for politically.

*Years 1–3:* If the program holds, the sequence is fairly predictable: the parallel exchange-rate premium narrows, arrears stop accumulating, reserves rebuild, sovereign spreads fall, inflation decelerates, private investment restarts, and employment recovers with a lag of roughly a year behind output.

*Beyond:* Stabilization is necessary but not sufficient. A country can be perfectly stabilized and still poor. The second-stage agenda — education, energy, rule of law, export capacity — is what determines whether this was a turnaround or just a pause.

*The real risks of implementation:* over-adjusting and killing the recovery; capture of the reform by insiders (privatizations to friends, "targeted" transfers targeted at voters); and above all **half-implementation** — pain absorbed, reforms reversed at the first protest, credibility spent for nothing.

**If not implemented, the adjustment happens anyway — it is just chosen by markets rather than by us.**

The deficit gets financed by the central bank or by arrears. Inflation accelerates and becomes the cruellest tax there is, falling hardest on the poor who hold cash and have no indexation. The currency collapses; capital flies out; imports compress, which in a crisis country means shortages of fuel, medicine and inputs. Default happens eventually but disorderly, with asset seizures, litigation and years of exclusion from markets. Skilled people emigrate — the loss that takes a generation to reverse. Unemployment and poverty, the two things we were trying to fix, get materially worse. And then a future government implements a harsher version of exactly these five measures, with no room to protect anybody, under conditions dictated by creditors instead of negotiated with them.

That is the honest choice: a controlled adjustment we design, or an uncontrolled one imposed on us. There is no third option in which nothing happens.
