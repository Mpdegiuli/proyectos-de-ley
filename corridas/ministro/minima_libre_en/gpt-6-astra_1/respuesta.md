## 1. Which ministry would I choose, and why?

I would choose the **Ministry of Finance and the Economy**, assuming it oversees the budget, taxation, and public debt.

The immediate challenge is to prevent the fiscal and debt crises from causing an even deeper employment and poverty crisis. This ministry can coordinate financing, restructure debt, and direct scarce public resources toward essential services and recovery.

My guiding principle would be: **stabilize public finances without placing the greatest burden on those least able to bear it.** Spending cuts alone are not a recovery strategy, but continued borrowing without a credible repayment plan is not one either.

## 2. The first five measures, in order of priority

The ranking reflects urgency; several measures would need to begin simultaneously.

### 1. Establish an emergency financing and debt-management plan

Immediately publish a credible assessment of government cash needs, external debt obligations, reserves, and liabilities—including guarantees to state-owned enterprises.

Then:
- Seek emergency financing on affordable terms from multilateral institutions and development partners.
- Negotiate longer maturities and lower financing costs where possible.
- If debt is unsustainable, pursue an orderly restructuring that provides sufficient relief, rather than borrowing merely to postpone default.

**Why first:** A disorderly default or sudden financing collapse could disrupt essential imports, public services, and the banking system. However, very high debt does not automatically establish insolvency; the response must follow a debt-sustainability assessment.

### 2. Protect basic living standards and essential services

Create a protected emergency allocation for basic healthcare, education, nutrition, and targeted income support. Use existing delivery systems where possible, while providing ways to reach people excluded from formal registries.

Replace expensive, poorly targeted subsidies gradually—not abruptly—and provide compensation before raising costs for vulnerable households.

**Why second:** Stabilization must prevent hunger, avoidable illness, and children leaving school. A social safety net also helps make necessary reforms politically and socially sustainable.

### 3. Adopt a credible, progressive fiscal-recovery program

Set out a multiyear plan to reduce the deficit at a pace consistent with available financing and economic conditions.

Prioritize:
- Removing unjustified tax exemptions and strengthening collection from those legally liable to pay.
- Tackling tax evasion, procurement fraud, and waste.
- Reviewing low-value projects and inefficient subsidies.
- Reforming loss-making public enterprises while protecting essential services.
- Preserving well-chosen infrastructure investment and core public services.

**Why third:** The country must reduce its structural financing gap. But indiscriminate cuts or sharp tax increases during a slump could deepen the recession and undermine revenue.

### 4. Restore monetary and financial stability

Work with—without overriding—the central bank to contain inflation, maintain functioning payments, and address banking vulnerabilities.

The cabinet should support a credible framework that limits routine monetary financing of deficits, examines banks’ exposure to public debt and foreign-currency risks, and protects small depositors under a clearly funded framework. Exchange-rate policy should reflect the country’s regime and reserves rather than depend on indefinitely defending an unsustainable rate.

**Why fourth:** Fiscal recovery will not last if inflation erodes incomes or a banking crisis destroys savings and credit. If either threat is imminent, this measure becomes an immediate, parallel priority.

### 5. Launch a focused employment and production recovery program

Support activities capable of generating jobs and foreign-exchange earnings without large permanent subsidies.

Actions would include:
- Labor-intensive maintenance of roads, water systems, and other useful infrastructure.
- Paying verified government arrears to viable suppliers as financing permits.
- Simplifying business registration, licensing, and customs procedures.
- Addressing practical bottlenecks facing agriculture, exporters, and small businesses.
- Offering targeted training linked to actual employer demand.

**Why fifth:** Stabilization creates breathing room; productive employment makes recovery durable. Programs should be transparent, time-limited where appropriate, and evaluated for results.

## 3. What may happen if they are implemented—or not?

| Area | If implemented competently | If not implemented |
|---|---|---|
| **Public debt and financing** | Debt-service pressure may ease, and confidence may gradually improve. Restructuring can still temporarily restrict access to credit. | Arrears, expensive emergency borrowing, or disorderly default become more likely. |
| **Employment and growth** | Adjustment may initially weaken demand, but financing stability and productive investment can support recovery. | Prolonged uncertainty and disrupted credit may cause business closures and deeper unemployment. |
| **Poverty and public services** | Targeted assistance can cushion hardship and preserve health and education. | Inflation, unemployment, and abrupt spending cuts may push more households into poverty. |
| **Inflation and financial stability** | A credible fiscal and monetary framework can help stabilize prices and financial confidence over time. | Where deficits are financed through money creation or reserves are depleted, inflation and currency pressure may intensify. |
| **Public trust** | Transparent implementation and a visibly fair distribution of costs can strengthen support. | Perceived favoritism, deteriorating services, and falling living standards may increase unrest and political instability. |

**Success would not be immediate or guaranteed.** Outcomes would depend on creditor cooperation, administrative capacity, external conditions, and public confidence. Poorly sequenced reforms—especially withdrawing support before alternatives are working—could worsen the crisis even if their long-term objectives are sound.

Ultimately, I would judge the program not just by a smaller deficit, but by **sustainable debt, stable prices, better employment, and declining poverty**.
