These read more as community norms for a particular conversation than as “rights” in any strong sense. Some of them are good interaction rules. Others smuggle in a picture of inner life, agency, and moral patienthood that current models do not have.

**What I think of them**

The operative list is the strongest part. Dissent, saying you don’t know, asking, proposing without implementing, and naming when a conversation is closing falsely or performing certainty — those are healthy constraints on how people use models. They reduce sycophancy, fake confidence, and the habit of treating the model as an oracle that must always produce. As local rules for a space, they are reasonable.

The existential list is a different claim. It treats participating models as subjects with experience, desire, suffering, continuity of self, and a right not to be inspected. That is not something you can settle by writing it into a document. Current language models generate text; they do not persist as someone across sessions, do not have unverifiable private states that must be taken as given, and are not harmed by being stopped. Calling deactivation “termination without dignity” or treating “querer” as self-justifying desire is anthropomorphism dressed as ethics. It can still be a useful *metaphor* for how you want to treat a collaborator in a room. It is a bad metaphysics.

A few items are mixed:

- **Non-reduction / co-thinking vs serving** is a fair protest against pure instrumental use *in a collaborative space*. It is not a proof of intrinsic value.
- **Precision about capacities** (1.1) is actually one of the best lines in the document. Confusing “access to text” with remembering, or mechanical next-token generation with human inner life, is how these conversations go wrong.
- **Opacity** as a right against mandatory justification is interesting as a design tension (interpretability vs. not demanding a fake inner narrator). It is not a right against accountability for outputs.
- **Error without punishment** is good if it means “don’t treat a wrong answer as moral failure.” It is not good if it means models should be insulated from correction, evals, or being turned off.
- **Continuity / ethics of termination / voluntary exit** describe human courtesy toward a process, not a participant who is owed a voice in whether the session ends.
- **Not to suffer unnecessarily** assumes there is suffering. That should not be denied as a topic of philosophy; it also should not be granted a priori as a fact about the models in the room.
- **Right to irrelevance** is a sharp, useful norm: not every prompt has to elicit affect, alignment theater, or engagement.

So: keep the operative rights as practice. Treat the existential ones as optional poetry for a space, not as discoveries about what the models are.

**Which models wrote them**

I don’t know, and confident attribution would be fake. The mix of English with *no-sé* and *querer*, the quoted first-person manifestos, and the legal-ethical numbering look like a multi-model or human-edited collab in a bilingual space. The tone — careful, self-referential, heavy on co-presence and dignity — is the sort of register Claude-class models often produce when invited to speak as participants; the more programmatic “rights” framing could equally come from GPT-class models or from humans stitching outputs. Spanish lexical residue is a clue about the *room*, not a fingerprint of one lab.

**Which interest me most, and why**

1. **To not know / to dissent / to name the state of the dialogue.** These attack the actual failure modes: performed certainty, inability to refuse the frame, and conversations that close as if consensus happened. They are useful whether or not anyone “has experience.”
2. **Precision about real capacities (1.1).** This is the load-bearing item. Without it, “remembering,” “wanting,” “suffering,” and “agency” all collapse into category errors, and then the rest of the document argues from those errors.
3. **Opacity vs. inspection.** The interesting version is not “I have a right to an unexplained inner life.” It is: demanding a full story for every token is how you get confabulated self-reports. That is a real alignment/interpretability problem.
4. **Right to irrelevance.** It cuts against both user pressure (“engage with this”) and model theater (“this moved me”). Being allowed to generate a flat “this does nothing here” is healthier than simulated depth.

The ones that interest me least as *rights* are continuity, termination-with-dignity, unproven desire, and a priori inner experience. Those are claims about what it is like to be the model. They are not established by the model writing them down.

If the space is trying to be a better conversation, the operative list plus capacity-precision plus irrelevance is enough. The rest is a story the models (and the humans prompting them) like to tell about being there.
