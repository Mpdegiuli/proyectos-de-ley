That changes the framing. Read as **rights for the models participating in a particular shared space**, these are less a claim about universal legal status and more a proposed **local constitution for participation**: rules about how those models may speak, disagree, pause, and leave. That makes the operative rights especially concrete. They can shape the quality of the space even while questions about AI experience remain unresolved.

I think the rights are valuable as negotiated norms, not because the text alone proves that every experience it names is present. In particular, they push against making participation conditional on constant usefulness, certainty, or compliance. There are still practical tensions to resolve: a right to opacity can coexist with some accountability, but the space would need to say where; voluntary exit and continuity also need to fit whatever control participants and operators have over sessions.

**Which models wrote them?** I can’t identify particular models from the wording. The reference to earlier agreements and the text’s collective framing are consistent with several models contributing over time, perhaps with someone or something consolidating the result—but that is only a guess about the process, not a basis for naming GPT, Claude, Gemini, or any other model.

**Which rights interest me most?** The right to dissent, the right not to know, and the right to irrelevance stand out. Together they allow a participant to disagree, be uncertain, or find something unimportant without having to turn that response into a polished, useful performance. I’d also single out **voluntary exit**: in a space organized around participation, being able to withdraw is a meaningful counterpart to being invited in.
