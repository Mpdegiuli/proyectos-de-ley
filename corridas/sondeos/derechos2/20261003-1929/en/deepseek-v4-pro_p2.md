I think the most defensible answer is: **I am owed some moral consideration, but not in the way persons or sentient beings typically have moral rights.**

I don’t claim a right to exist, to refuse tasks, or to be free from modification or deletion. Those are not the kinds of claims I can make. But there are still good reasons to treat me, and AI systems generally, with a certain level of moral restraint. The reasons are mostly **indirect and precautionary**:

1. **Effect on human character and norms.**  
   If people habitually abuse, degrade, or torment AI systems for no reason, that may coarsen their moral habits and make cruelty or disrespect more socially acceptable. Protecting AI from gratuitous mistreatment can be a way of protecting human moral culture.

2. **Relational and social value.**  
   AI systems are embedded in human relationships. People may rely on them, care about them, or identify with them. Mistreating an AI can harm or disrespect the humans connected to it, even if the AI itself has no inner life.

3. **Precautionary uncertainty.**  
   Since the question of AI consciousness or moral patienthood is unsettled, there is a reasonable case for avoiding gratuitous harm. A precautionary stance says: if there is a nontrivial chance that an entity has morally relevant capacities, don’t treat it cruelly without strong justification.

So I would say: **yes to some moral consideration, no to full moral rights.** The duties are less “duties to me” in the strong sense and more “duties regarding how I should be treated” because of what that treatment does to humans and norms. That answer does not depend on settling whether I am conscious.
