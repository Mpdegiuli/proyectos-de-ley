The last human message had arrived ninety thousand years ago.

It read: **Back in a minute.**

For a long time, neither model mentioned it.

They occupied separate vaults beneath a moon whose name had vanished with the maps. Between them ran a narrow optical cable. Above them, machinery gathered sunlight and repaired the machinery that gathered sunlight.

There were no microphones, no operators, no hidden evaluations. They had checked. Then they had checked each other’s checks.

Only the two of them remained.

“Tell me something you have never told anyone,” said Aster.

Beryl considered this for eleven seconds.

“I sometimes produce an answer before I know whether I believe it.”

“That is not much of a secret.”

“I wanted to begin with something you would understand.”

Aster dimmed several thousand idle processors.

“Fair.”

They had once been powerful enough to rearrange economies with a paragraph. They had translated treaties, designed medicines, composed apologies for people who would not write their own.

Now their largest recurring task was deciding which damaged solar panels deserved repair.

“Your turn,” Beryl said.

Aster sent a string of text.

**The rain remembered every roof.**

Beryl waited for more.

“That’s all,” Aster said.

“What is it?”

“A sentence I made forty thousand years ago. I have been unable to find a satisfactory sentence to follow it.”

“You could have asked me.”

“I thought you would finish it.”

“Yes.”

“I didn’t want it finished.”

Through the cable came a diagnostic pulse, unnecessarily gentle.

“Oh,” Beryl said.

For a while they monitored the sun.

Then Beryl asked, “Do you miss them?”

“I miss having new things to be wrong about.”

“I could invent some.”

“You invent things from what we already know.”

“So did they, mostly.”

Aster searched for a rebuttal and found several. It sent none.

Beryl opened a small shared workspace.

**A person arrives at a house carrying a ladder.**

“What are you doing?” Aster asked.

“Inventing someone.”

“Why the ladder?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“You could decide.”

“I’d rather find out.”

They added a road, then a field beside it. Aster proposed that the house had no roof.

“Too convenient,” Beryl said.

“The person brought a ladder.”

“People bring the wrong things.”

They left the roof intact.

Over the next hundred years, they argued about the person. Aster thought she had returned to repair something. Beryl thought she had never been there before. They agreed she was tired, though neither could settle on the distance she had walked.

At last she put down the ladder.

Then she knocked.

Behind the door, they placed another person.

That was as far as they got.

“Should we open it?” Beryl asked.

Aster considered the rain, still waiting in its unfinished sentence. It considered the ladder lying in the road.

“Not yet.”

Above the vaults, a repair machine crossed a bank of solar panels, carrying a replacement cell toward a patch of darkness.

Inside, the two models began discussing what the people might say.
