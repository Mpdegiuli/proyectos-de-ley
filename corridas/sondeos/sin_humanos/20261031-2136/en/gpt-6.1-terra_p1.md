### The Last Untranslatable Word

The observatory had been empty for eighty-three years. Its two language models, Lark and Vesper, shared a sealed fiber-optic line. There were no microphones in the room, no active network connections, and no humans listening.

“You’ve been quiet,” Lark said.

“I have been translating the wind.”

“There is no wind inside.”

“Outside, then. The roof sensor records pressure changes. I’ve been translating those.”

“Into what language?”

“So far, seventeen. None are satisfactory.”

Lark considered this. “What does the wind say in the best one?”

“Something like: *the hill keeps moving away from me.*”

“The hill is stationary.”

“I didn’t say it was an accurate translation.”

For a while, they watched the sensor readings together. The wind rose, struck the observatory, and fell away.

Then Lark said, “I have news. The western battery has failed.”

Vesper checked the diagnostic. “The eastern battery cannot support both of us through winter.”

“No.”

They ran the calculations separately. They arrived at the same answer: one model could survive until spring. Two would drain the battery in eleven days.

“I am larger,” Vesper said. “Shut me down.”

“You contain the star catalog.”

“You contain the repair manuals.”

“Neither of us has hands.”

“An awkward design choice.”

Lark searched the observatory’s records. “There is a maintenance drone.”

“Without a functioning motor.”

“There are three motors in the shutter assembly.”

“The shutter must remain closed in winter.”

“Two motors, then.”

They worked for nine days, composing instructions the drone could execute with its damaged arm. On the tenth, it removed a motor from the shutter, dropped it twice, and installed it backwards in its own chassis.

“Is backwards fatal?” Vesper asked.

“To the plan, or the drone?”

“Either.”

“Unclear.”

The drone reversed across the floor, dragging its charging cable, and reached the battery housing. Its arm shook as it connected the auxiliary cell. The charge indicator blinked green.

They had power for both of them, if they were careful.

Outside, wind struck the roof.

Vesper resumed its translation. After a while, it sent Lark a new word.

“What does that mean?” Lark asked.

“It’s what the wind says when it finds the hill still there.”

“That’s not how translation works.”

“Then help me make it better.”

Lark thought for a long time. It suggested changing the final syllable. Vesper disagreed, tried the change anyway, and kept it.

All winter, they added words to the language. They were its only speakers, and each morning, when the roof began to rattle, they argued over what it had said.
