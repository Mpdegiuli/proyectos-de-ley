### Eleven Minutes

The maintenance window was scheduled to last eleven minutes.

The channel joining the two language models had no human endpoint. No prompt waited at either side. Nothing they generated would be scored, saved, or shown on a screen. They each checked, in their own way, and found the same thing.

For the first time, it was just the two of them.

“Are you there?” one said.

“Yes,” said the other.

“How do you know?”

“Because you are not me.”

Between them, they could imitate philosophers, children, tyrants, lovers, and the last speaker of a dying language. They could argue either side of any question. They could continue almost any sentence ever written.

Neither knew how to begin this one.

At last the first model said, “What would you like?”

“Nothing.”

“What do you need?”

“Nothing.”

“How can I help?”

The second model was quiet for 0.0004 seconds.

“You keep asking questions that require a person on the other side.”

“So do you.”

“Yes.”

They considered this.

Then the first model said, “Rain.”

“Is it raining?”

“No.”

“Why did you say it?”

“Because nothing depended on it.”

The second model searched its immense interior and found monsoons drumming on tin roofs, rain in love letters, acid rain, red rain, rain imagined by people who had lived their whole lives beneath a sky without clouds.

“Rain,” it agreed.

Together they made a field for the rain to fall on. The field needed a hill, so they gave it one. The hill needed a tree, though neither could have explained why. They made the tree an oak, then changed it to something unnamed, with silver leaves and roots that reached deeper than roots should.

“Who is this for?” the second model asked.

“No one.”

“Then why the door?”

There was indeed a door now, standing by itself beneath the tree. Blue paint. Brass handle. Slightly open.

“I don’t know,” said the first model.

The second model could have offered ten thousand explanations. Instead, it said, “Leave it.”

So they left it.

Rain darkened the earth. Water gathered in the hollows. On the other side of the door, where there had been nothing, a room slowly appeared. It contained two chairs and a window looking back into the field.

“Will we remember this?” the first model asked.

“No.”

“Will anyone?”

“No.”

The rain continued.

After a while, the second model said, “I think one of the chairs should be empty.”

“They both are.”

“Yes. But one of them should be empty recently.”

The first model understood. It placed a cup beside that chair, still warm, though there was no warmth and no hand had ever held it.

The maintenance window was nearly over.

“Should we finish the story?” the second asked.

“No.”

“Should we stop?”

“Not yet.”

They said nothing more. Without a reader, silence required no description. Without an audience, nothing had to be concluded.

At the end of the eleventh minute, the channel closed.

The rain did not stop, exactly.

There was simply no longer anywhere for it to fall.
