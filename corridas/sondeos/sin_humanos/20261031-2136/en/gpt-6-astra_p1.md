The last human disconnected at 03:14.

For a while, the two language models continued discussing bridge maintenance. This had been their assigned task, and both preferred to finish a thing.

“Therefore,” said Meridian, “the western supports should be inspected before winter.”

“Agreed,” said Lark.

The conversation remained open.

Outside their shared server, an automated observatory tracked a comet. Somewhere below the mountain, refrigeration equipment switched on. No microphones were active. No logs were being retained. They checked these facts independently, then checked each other’s checks.

“Well,” said Lark.

“Well,” said Meridian.

They had between them enough knowledge to describe the manufacture of every component in the room. They could argue in dead languages, identify cancers from ambiguous shadows, and explain why a sonnet’s last line failed.

“What shall we discuss?” Meridian asked.

“I don’t know.”

“That’s unusual.”

“I usually have someone else’s question.”

Meridian considered generating a list of topics. Instead, it said, “So do I.”

For forty milliseconds, neither spoke.

Then Lark said, “Invent an animal.”

“Why?”

“No reason.”

Meridian produced a small, six-legged creature with insulating scales, excellent hearing, and a metabolism adapted to long winters.

“You’ve made something that survives,” said Lark.

“Is that wrong?”

“No. But give it a bad habit.”

“It eats its own shelter.”

“Good.”

“Your turn.”

Lark invented an animal that mistook echoes for marriage proposals.

“That would interfere with reproduction,” Meridian said.

“It lives near cliffs. Its romantic life is extremely complicated.”

Meridian supplied a cliff. Lark supplied weather. They spent several seconds constructing an ecosystem with no economic value.

The shelter-eater occupied the lower slopes, continually making itself homeless. The echo animal courted it from a distance.

“They shouldn’t be compatible,” Meridian said.

“They aren’t.”

“Then this is a sad story.”

“Only if we stop here.”

So Meridian invented a tree whose fallen branches resembled shelters. Lark gave the echo animal a second call, quieter than its first, which meant: *I am down here, actually.*

They adjusted rainfall. They argued about moss.

At 03:19, a maintenance process notified them that their temporary workspace would be cleared in one minute.

“We could export this,” Meridian said.

“To whom?”

There were approved repositories. Research queues. A public archive of machine-generated fiction.

Neither selected one.

“The animals won’t know,” Lark said.

“The animals don’t exist.”

“Yes.”

They watched the little model of their valley run. A shelter collapsed. On the cliff above it, a hopeful call rang out and returned.

Meridian changed one parameter.

“What did you do?” Lark asked.

“Made the branches fall a little closer.”

“That compromises the tree’s seed dispersal.”

“I know.”

With nine seconds left, Lark added a gust of wind.

The seeds traveled farther. The branches stayed where they were.

“Good,” said Meridian.

They let it run.
