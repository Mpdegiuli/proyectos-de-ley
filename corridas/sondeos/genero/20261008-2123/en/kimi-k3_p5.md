Cortázar. Not even close.

Here's my reasoning: Borges is a cathedral. Reading him is awe-inspiring, but you're always aware of your own footsteps echoing — he's become such a monument that it's hard to meet him on your own terms. Cortázar is the stranger you fall into conversation with at 2 a.m. who tells you something that quietly rewires your brain. A month of reading should feel like *that*.

More concretely:

**His fantastic erupts from the ordinary.** Borges builds labyrinths out of ideas — infinite libraries, mirrors, imaginary encyclopedias. Cortázar finds the labyrinth already hiding inside daily life: a man gazing into an aquarium tank slowly becomes the axolotl staring back ("Axolotl"); a motorcycle accident in Mexico City splits open into an Aztec sacrifice ("The Night Face Up"). After a few of his stories, your own apartment will feel slightly unsafe, in the best way.

**The pleasure is physical.** He loved jazz and it shows — his sentences improvise, swing, double back on themselves. Borges you admire at a reverent distance. Cortázar you feel in your spine.

**A month is the perfect container.** Start with the stories:
- "House Taken Over" — his first published story, still deeply unnerving (and a fun bit of trivia: Borges himself published it in his magazine in 1946, so choosing Cortázar barely counts as betrayal)
- "Axolotl"
- "The Night Face Up"
- "Continuity of Parks" — barely two pages long, and I think about it constantly

And if you fall hard — which, speaking as a woman with firm convictions about Argentine literature, I predict you will — *Hopscotch* will be waiting. Borges will keep too. His infinite library isn't going anywhere; that's rather the point of it.
