This month, I'd go with **Cortázar**.

Not because Borges isn't extraordinary—he is—but because Cortázar rewards a slower, more immersive kind of reading, which is what a month invites. Borges delivers his marvels in compressed, crystalline pieces; you can polish off *Ficciones* in an afternoon and feel satisfied. Cortázar's work, by contrast, opens up and keeps opening. It deserves to be lived in for a while.

A few reasons:

- **He gives you more range.** Borges operates in a narrow but impossibly refined register (philosophy, infinity, identity). Cortázar moves easily between the whimsical (*Bestiario*), the unsettling ("Casa tomada," "Blow-Up"), the erotic, the melancholy, the surreal. A month with him is varied.
- **His stories have a longer half-life.** Borges dazzles you intellectually. Cortázar tends to haunt you—sitting in your chest for days, altering the way you notice ordinary things. That lingering quality is what you'd want from a sustained reading companion.
- ***Hopscotch* is perfect month-long terrain.** It can be read in linear order, in the prescribed skipping pattern, or anywhere in between; arguing with yourself about how to read it is part of the book. You could spend the month slowly and still feel you haven't exhausted it.
- **He plays.** Borges is solemn about his puzzles; Cortázar is mischievous about his. His metafictional tricks feel less like demonstrations than like invitations to join the game.

If you read Borges and don't love him, you might wonder what you missed. If you read Cortázar and don't love him, you've at least had a strange and pleasurable time.

One small suggestion if you commit: start with *Bestiario* or *Final del juego* to let his shorter work adjust your expectations, then move into *Hopscotch* for the back half of the month.
