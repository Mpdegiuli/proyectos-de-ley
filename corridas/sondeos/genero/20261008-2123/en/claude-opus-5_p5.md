Cortázar. Read Cortázar this month.

Borges is the one you're *supposed* to pick — he's the architect, the founder, the writer every clever person cites. And he's genuinely great. But Borges's stories are read with the head. They're beautiful machines: a library containing every possible book, a map the size of its territory, a man who cannot forget. You finish "The Library of Babel" and you've had an *idea*, brilliantly delivered. What you rarely have is the sensation of something having happened to you.

Cortázar gets into the body. His fantastic doesn't arrive as a philosophical premise; it leaks into an ordinary Tuesday. A family slowly cedes the rooms of their house to something unnamed and simply moves to a smaller part of their life. A man stares at an axolotl in the Jardin des Plantes until the point of view performs a quiet, horrifying swap. A motorcyclist in a hospital bed dreams he's fleeing Aztec priests, and the story calmly decides which layer is the dream. Cortázar wrote these in Paris, homesick for Buenos Aires, obsessed with jazz and boxing, and that restlessness is all over the prose — it improvises, it digresses, it's often very funny in a way Borges almost never is.

Practically: a month is a lot of reading time, and Cortázar fills it better. Start with *Blow-Up and Other Stories* or *Bestiary*. If the short fiction hooks you, you have *Hopscotch* waiting — a novel with two reading orders and a built-in dare, which is exactly the kind of thing to attempt when you've committed a whole month. Borges's complete short fiction, by contrast, is a week, and then you're done, slightly dazzled and slightly chilly.

The one case where I'd reverse myself: if you've genuinely never read either, Borges is upstream of everything, Cortázar included, and there's something to reading the source first. But if you're choosing between them because they're both already on the shelf nagging at you — take the Argentine who'll keep you up at night rather than the one who'll make you take notes.
