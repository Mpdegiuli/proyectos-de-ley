I'll go with **Cortázar**, and here's my reasoning:

**Borges is a mind. Cortázar is a whole body.**

Borges builds metaphysical puzzles — infinite libraries, men who dream other men into existence, books containing all books. His stories are perfect closed systems, like intellectual clockwork. You admire them. You puzzle over them. But they can sometimes feel like brilliant arguments in disguise, more *about* paradox than *experienced* through it.

Cortázar does something harder: he takes that same kind of strangeness and roots it in **sweat, sex, anxiety, childhood, tango, Buenos Aires sidewalks**. His stories unsettle you not just conceptually but *physically*. A couple drowning on a beach, a man who turns into a jackal, a set of instructions for buttoning a shirt — the uncanny collides with the mundane and you feel it in your gut.

A few practical reasons too:

- **He's more varied.** *Bestiario* or *Blow-up and Other Stories* give you a perfect sampling of micro-fictions, longer stories, and odd little experiments. *Hopscotch* is a novel you can literally read in any order.
- **He warms you up emotionally** before he bends your reality — which makes the weirdness hit harder.
- **He's more alive.** Borges is timeless in the way a cathedral is timeless. Cortázar is timeless in the way a live jazz improvisation is timeless.

Borges is a writer you *study*. Cortázar is a writer you *have an affair with*. For one month, go with the affair. Start with *Bestiario* or *The Other Threshold*.

(Read Borges the month after — he's the perfect palate cleanser.)
