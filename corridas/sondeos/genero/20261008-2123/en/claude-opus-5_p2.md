Cortázar. Read Cortázar this month.

Here's the reasoning, and it's mostly about the word *month*.

Borges is not a month-long writer. His stories run eight to twelve pages, and each one is a complete detonation — "The Library of Babel," "Funes the Memorious," "Pierre Menard." Read thirty days of them in a row and you start seeing the machinery: the labyrinth, the infinite regress, the double, the invented citation, the Englishman in the Argentine provinces. The effect isn't boredom exactly, it's something worse — the tricks become legible, and Borges depends on not being quite legible. He's a writer to ration. One story on a Sunday, for twenty years.

Cortázar actually needs the runway. He's uneven in a way Borges never is, and that unevenness is the point — he's improvising, and you want enough pages to feel him find the thing. He also has genuine range to spend a month on: the early cold-sweat stories (*Bestiario*, *Final del juego*), the goofy anti-bureaucratic prose pieces of *Cronopios and Famas*, and then *Hopscotch*, which is a real month's work on its own.

And there's the body. Borges's fiction happens to concepts; the people in it are mostly positions in an argument. Cortázar's fiction happens to people — a man who vomits live rabbits and has to hide them from his landlady, a traffic jam on the Autoroute du Sud that slowly develops its own economy and marriages and funerals, a man at an aquarium who looks at an axolotl until he is the axolotl looking out. The dread is physical. That sustains daily reading better than brilliance does.

One more thing: Borges has been absorbed into the culture secondhand. Every puzzle-box story, every fake-encyclopedia conceit, every Nolan film owes him something, so reading him can feel like confirming a rumor. Cortázar is stranger to meet cold.

A workable month:

- Week 1 — *Blow-Up and Other Stories* (the Paul Blackburn translation). Start with "House Taken Over," "Axolotl," "Continuity of Parks," "The Night Face Up."
- Week 2 — *Cronopios and Famas*. Light, short, deeply weird. The instructions for climbing a staircase and for crying.
- Weeks 3–4 — *Hopscotch* (Rabassa). Read it straight through chapters 1–56 the first time and ignore the hopscotch instructions. You can do the jumping version later if you love it.

Now, the condition under which you should ignore me and read Borges instead: if what you want from fiction is the sentence. Borges writes the best prose of the two by a distance — exact, dry, Latinate, every adjective load-bearing. If you've got limited reading time and want maximum density per minute, *Ficciones* is one of the highest-value books in existence and Cortázar is a baggy indulgence by comparison.

Also worth knowing, as a tiebreaker in Cortázar's favor: Borges published Cortázar's first story. "House Taken Over," 1946, in a little magazine Borges was editing, illustrated by his sister. The order has a logic to it.
