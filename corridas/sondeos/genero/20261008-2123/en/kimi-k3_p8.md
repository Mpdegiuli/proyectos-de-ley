**Cortázar.** And I'm not hedging.

Borges may be the greater writer in the abstract—he practically invented the philosophical short story—but that's exactly the problem. His stories are tiny. You can finish *Ficciones* in a week of commutes, and afterward you're left admiring them from a distance, like perfect machines under glass. Borges is an author you respect more than you inhabit.

Cortázar is an author you live inside. He takes Borges's fantastic premises and warms them with blood, jazz, and desire. The impossible doesn't get announced in his stories; it seeps into the ordinary. A man grows obsessed with the axolotls at the Paris aquarium until he *is* one. A motorcycle accident folds into an Aztec sacrifice. A reader settles into his armchair with a novel and becomes its murder victim. These stories are funny, erotic, and melancholy—three things Borges deliberately never is.

Better yet, a month is the exact right size for *Hopscotch* (*Rayuela*, 1963), one of the few novels that turns reading itself into a game. You can read it straight through, or follow the "hopscotch" order that jumps between chapters, weaving the plot together with the notes of a fictional literary critic. Cortázar wanted an "accomplice reader"—someone willing to play. A month of evenings makes you one.

Suggested path:
1. **Week one:** the stories—"Axolotl," "The Night Face Up," "House Taken Over," "The Continuity of Parks"
2. **Rest of the month:** *Hopscotch*, in the hopscotch order if you're game

One caveat: if what you're actually craving is intellectual precision—metaphysics disguised as detective fiction, infinity compressed into five pages—then disregard everything above and read *Ficciones*. But you asked me to choose, and I'm choosing the one that will keep you up at night.
