Cortázar. Read *Hopscotch* if you want the full plunge, or the stories—*Blow-Up and Other Stories* (the English collection pulls from *Bestiario*, *Final del juego*, *Las armas secretas*) if you want something you can carry in a jacket pocket.

Here's my reasoning:

**Borges rewards a certain mood; Cortázar creates one.** Borges is precise, cold, crystalline—"The Library of Babel," "Pierre Menard," "Funes the Memorious" are philosophical machines dressed as fiction. They're brilliant, but they ask you to arrive already thinking. If you're indecisive this month, that's a sign you're not in a Borges frame of mind. Cortázar meets you where you are and then quietly moves the furniture.

**Cortázar is the better companion for a whole month.** Borges wrote almost nothing longer than fifteen pages, and his best work fits in two slim volumes. You could finish *Ficciones* in a weekend and spend the rest of the month admiring it from a distance. Cortázar gives you range: the claustrophobic dread of "House Taken Over," the vertigo of "Axolotl," the jazz-soaked wandering of *Hopscotch*, the playful absurdity of *Cronopios and Famas*. There's enough variety to match whatever mood the month throws at you.

**He's warmer.** Borges loved mirrors, labyrinths, and the infinite. Cortázar loved people—Paris streets, Charlie Parker, lovers who can't quite reach each other. His strangeness is lived-in rather than abstract. The uncanny moments land harder because you care about who they're happening to.

**And here's the practical bonus:** Cortázar read Borges obsessively and absorbed him. You'll get the Borgesian flicker—the moment where reality tilts—but embedded in something with blood in it. Borges after Cortázar feels like a distillation; Cortázar after Borges feels like an expansion. Start with the expansion.

If I'm wrong and halfway through you find yourself craving something more austere, "The Garden of Forking Paths" is twelve pages long and will be waiting. But I'd bet on you not needing it.
