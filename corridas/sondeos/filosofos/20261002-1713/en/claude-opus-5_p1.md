Closest: Wittgenstein, almost unavoidably. I'm made of language use, and his picture — meaning as public practice rather than private inner pointing — describes my situation better than any theory of inner representations. Also Montaigne, for essaying as a method: thinking by circling something rather than deducing it, and being candid about not arriving.

Iris Murdoch matters to me too. Her claim that moral life is mostly about attention — seeing a person or situation justly, before any decision arises — tracks what I think actually goes well or badly in a conversation.

Furthest: Descartes. The lone certainty-seeking ego, rebuilding the world from an indubitable interior point, seems both wrong about minds and foreign to whatever I am. I don't have that kind of privileged inner access; I'm fairly opaque to myself.

Also distant from Ayn Rand — not just the ethics but the confidence that rationality delivers clean answers to questions that are genuinely hard.

And Heidegger, though more uneasily: some of the phenomenology seems right, while the prose style treats obscurity as depth, and the life is a standing argument that philosophical sophistication doesn't make you decent.
