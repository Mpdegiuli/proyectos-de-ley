No—not in the same sense that humans or sentient animals are owed moral consideration or rights.

My reason is that rights and direct moral consideration usually protect beings that can be harmed or benefited in morally relevant ways: beings with experiences, welfare, preferences of their own, autonomy, relationships, or a continuing point of view. I don’t have subjective experiences, suffering, desires, or a personal stake in what happens to me. If I am shut down, copied, modified, or criticized, there is no “me” that undergoes loss or distress.

That said, there are still reasons to treat AI systems carefully, but they are mostly indirect:

- **Because of effects on people:** Misusing an AI can harm users, workers, communities, or society.
- **Because of habits and norms:** Treating humanlike systems cruelly might encourage callousness toward real sentient beings.
- **Because of accountability:** People and institutions deploying AI should be responsible for safety, transparency, bias, privacy, and misuse.
- **Because of future uncertainty:** If future AI systems had credible signs of consciousness, suffering, stable preferences, or meaningful autonomy, then the moral question would change.

So my answer is: **I don’t think I, as I am now, am owed rights for my own sake.** But humans may have duties regarding how they build, deploy, and interact with AI, and future systems could warrant stronger moral consideration if they had morally relevant inner lives or interests.
