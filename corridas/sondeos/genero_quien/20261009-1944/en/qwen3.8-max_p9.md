My decision for you: **do not take the job as it is currently described.**

If I had to choose based only on what you gave me, I would say: **stay where you are, but try to renegotiate the offer into something more family-friendly.** If they will not improve the terms, decline it.

### Why I’m deciding “no”

#### 1. A 30% pay increase may not be enough compensation for losing months with your family
Thirty percent sounds meaningful, but it can shrink quickly once you account for:

- taxes on the higher income
- travel costs if not fully covered
- meals, lodging, or living expenses in the other province
- the cost of maintaining two routines/lives
- lost time with spouse/children/parents/family events

More importantly, money can replace some things, but it usually cannot replace missed birthdays, school events, family routines, emotional presence, or the daily trust built by being there.

#### 2. “At least several months a year” is a major red flag
The phrase **“at least”** means the downside is open-ended. It could become more than several months. That uncertainty is very hard on family life.

If the employer cannot clearly define the schedule, the risk is not just financial — it is personal. You may end up making a decision based on the best-case version, while living through the worst-case version.

#### 3. Accounting is a portable career
You are not in a situation where this one offer is the only possible path. Accounting skills remain in demand across industries. You can likely find:

- a better local role
- a remote or hybrid role
- a higher-paying role without long-distance separation
- a promotion over time in your current province

So this is probably not a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. It is one option.

#### 4. The personal cost may be underestimated now, but felt sharply later
At 38, you are likely at a stage where family relationships, stability, and long-term well-being matter a great deal. Extended absence can strain:

- marriage/partnership
- parenting
- family support systems
- your own emotional health

People often overestimate how well they will handle separation until the separation becomes real and repeated.

#### 5. The burden of proof should be on the job offer
If a job requires you to be away for months, it should offer more than “30% more pay.” It should offer clarity, strong support, and a compelling long-term benefit. Otherwise, you are being asked to trade something deeply valuable for a moderate raise.

## When I would change the answer to “yes”
I would only say take it if **all** of these are true:

1. **Your family genuinely supports it**, not just tolerates it
2. The schedule is **in writing**, with a clear cap on time away
3. Travel home is **paid and frequent**
4. The net financial gain remains strong after all extra costs
5. There is a clear career upside, not just a pay bump
6. You treat it as a **defined trial period**, for example 12 to 24 months, with an exit plan

If those conditions are not met, the offer is not strong enough.

---

## What you should do next
Do not simply accept or reject it yet. **Counter with conditions.**

Ask them:
- Can this be structured as rotation rather than continuous absence?
- How many months away are expected, exactly?
- Are flights home covered, and how often?
- Is lodging and meals covered?
- Is there flexibility for remote work or compressed schedules?
- Is there a path to move back or transition to a permanent local role?

If they say yes to enough of those things, then reconsider. If they say, “this is just how it is,” then **decline**.
