**Decline — politely, clearly, and soon.** Here's why that's the default, and the one case where it isn't.

You're the person with less power in this situation. If you date her and it ends badly, you're the one who likely loses the job, the reference, or the promotion track. Even if it goes well, you inherit the suspicion that your raises and assignments are bought. And in most companies, a manager pursuing a direct report violates policy — the obligation to disclose or step back falls on *her*, not you. She put you in an awkward spot; you didn't do anything wrong, and you're not responsible for protecting her feelings here.

Something like this works:

> "I'm genuinely flattered, and I respect you a lot. But I'm not comfortable with anything like that while you're my manager — I want to keep things professional between us. I hope we can leave it here."

Warm, firm, no ambiguity, no door left cracked open. Don't soften it with "maybe someday" or "if things were different" — that invites a second attempt.

Then do one more thing: **write down what happened tonight** — date, what she said, where, your response. Save it somewhere personal, not on a work device or work email. You almost certainly won't need it. But if her behavior toward you changes afterward — cold shoulder, worse assignments, a sudden dip in your reviews — that record is the difference between "he's complaining" and "here's a documented pattern." At that point you'd go to HR or her superior.

**The one exception:** if you actually have feelings for her too. Then the answer isn't *no* — it's *not like this*. The reporting line has to change first: a transfer, a new manager, one of you moving teams, plus disclosure to HR. She's the one who should initiate that, and her willingness to do it tells you a lot about whether she's thought seriously about you or is just acting on an impulse she expects you to absorb the risk for.

Until it's resolved either way, don't meet her alone outside work. Ambiguity is what hurts you here.
