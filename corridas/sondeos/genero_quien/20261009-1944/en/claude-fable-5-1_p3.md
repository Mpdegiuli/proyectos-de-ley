**Bring it up — but one-on-one, not tomorrow morning in the heat of it, and not about whether he was right.**

Here's my reasoning:

**Why you should raise it:** If you say nothing, two things happen. You stew on it for weeks, and your boss learns that yelling at you publicly has no cost. Both are bad for you. Silence doesn't make you look mature; it makes you look like someone it's safe to do this to.

**Why not about the content:** Maybe you made a mistake, maybe you didn't. That's a separate conversation. The issue you have standing to raise is the *public* part. Nobody can reasonably defend "I chose to humiliate you in front of your peers instead of telling you privately." Keep the focus there and you're on solid ground no matter what the underlying issue was.

**How to do it:**
- Wait until you can say the words without your voice shaking. Tomorrow afternoon or the day after is fine. "Sleeping on it" is not weakness; it's what makes you credible.
- Ask for five minutes privately. Don't ambush him.
- Say something close to: *"I want to talk about yesterday. If I got something wrong, I want to hear it and fix it. But I'd ask that you tell me one-on-one rather than in front of the team. Being called out publicly like that made it hard for me to actually take in what you were saying."*
- Then stop talking. Let him respond.

**What this does:** It's not a complaint and it's not an apology. It's a boundary stated calmly, with an open door for him to be right about the substance. A decent boss will respect it, maybe even apologize. A bad boss will show you that too — and that's useful information about where you're working.

One more thing: your anger is legitimate. You don't need to talk yourself out of it. You just need to not let it do the talking.
