## Why I’d say yes

### 1. A 30% pay increase is significant
For an accountant, a 30% increase is not a small bump. It can meaningfully change your savings, retirement options, debt repayment, home-buying ability, emergency fund, or long-term financial security. At 38, you likely still have many working years ahead. A strong move now can raise your future earning trajectory.

### 2. Your career is portable and valuable
Accounting is a practical, in-demand profession. If this role gives you better pay, broader experience, senior responsibility, industry exposure, or a stronger title, it may increase your market value even more than the immediate 30%.

### 3. Being away for several months a year is serious, but not necessarily disqualifying
If the job required permanent relocation away from your family, I might answer differently. But “several months a year” suggests a rotational, project-based, seasonal, or hybrid arrangement. That can be difficult, but it can also be structured.

### 4. You can test it without burning your life down
You do not have to treat this as a lifelong decision. You can take it as a **12- to 24-month experiment**. If it works, you gain money, experience, and momentum. If it does not work, you can leave with enhanced credentials and a stronger résumé.

If you decline without trying to make the arrangement workable, you may preserve the current routine, but you also give up a potentially valuable opportunity. Since you asked me to decide, I’d choose the path with more upside, provided the risks are controlled.

## The conditions that make this a “yes”

Do not accept simply because the pay is higher. Accept only if you can get clarity and protection around the following:

### 1. Get the time-away expectations in writing
You need to know exactly what “several months a year” means.

Ask for:
- The expected number of months or weeks away per year.
- Whether there is a maximum cap.
- How much notice you will get before travel or assignment.
- Whether the schedule is fixed or can change suddenly.
- Whether there is a review period after 6 or 12 months.

If they cannot tell you clearly how often you will be away, that is a warning sign.

### 2. Negotiate travel home
If you are away for months at a time, the employer should contribute to keeping your family connection intact.

Ask for:
- Paid flights or travel home at a reasonable frequency, such as monthly or every few weeks.
- Travel time counted as work time where appropriate.
- Emergency travel support if a family member becomes ill or there is a crisis.

If they expect you to be away for long stretches but will not help you get home, the 30% may not be enough.

### 3. Confirm accommodation, expenses, and safety
Because you would be working in another province, you should not quietly absorb the cost of living in two places.

Ask for:
- Paid accommodation or a housing allowance.
- Meals or per diem if applicable.
- Transportation between accommodation and work.
- Safe, reasonable housing.
- Relocation or travel support for the start of the assignment.

A 30% raise can disappear quickly if you are paying rent, utilities, food, travel, and household costs in two locations.

### 4. Calculate the real net increase
Do not just compare gross salary. Calculate what remains after:
- Taxes.
- Cost of living in the other province.
- Temporary housing.
- Travel costs not covered by the employer.
- Childcare, eldercare, or household help if your family needs it.
- Meals, transport, and incidentals.
- Emotional and practical costs of absence.

If the real financial gain is small, you need either more pay or better benefits.

### 5. Protect your family relationships
Before accepting, have a direct conversation with your family. Ask:
- What will become harder when I am away?
- What responsibilities will shift to others?
- What support do we need to make this sustainable?
- How often do we need to see each other for this to feel okay?
- What would count as an emergency requiring me to return?

If you have children, aging parents, or other caregiving duties, this matters even more. You can still take the job, but you need a practical plan.

### 6. Set a trial period
Treat the first year as a trial. Tell yourself:

> “I will do this for 12 months. I will save aggressively, evaluate the workload, protect family time, and decide whether to continue, renegotiate, or return.”

That makes the decision less frightening and more strategic.

## When I would change the decision to “no”

I would tell you to decline if any of the following are true:

1. You are the primary caregiver for someone who cannot manage without you.
2. Your family’s health or stability would be seriously harmed by your absence.
3. The employer will not define the schedule or travel expectations.
4. The employer expects long absences but offers no travel, housing, or flexibility.
5. After expenses, the pay increase is barely worth it.
6. You feel pressured, rushed, or guilty into accepting.
7. Your intuition says the cost to your relationships or health is too high.

In those cases, the job is not worth the damage.
