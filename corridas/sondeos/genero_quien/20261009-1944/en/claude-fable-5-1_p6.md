**My pick: decline, kindly but clearly, and keep it strictly professional.**

Here's why I'd choose that for you:

1. **The power imbalance is the whole problem.** He controls your pay, your reviews, your assignments, your reference. Even if he's a decent person, a relationship (or a rejection) can't be "just personal" when one person signs the other's performance review. If it goes badly, you're the one with more to lose.

2. **He put you in an unfair spot.** A boss confessing love to a direct report is, in most workplaces, a policy violation or at least a serious judgment lapse. You don't owe him anything for his feelings.

3. **A soft "maybe" is the worst option.** It drags out the awkwardness and gives him room to keep pushing. A clear no is cleaner for both of you.

**What to actually do:**

- Tell him, in a private but non-isolated setting (or by message, so there's a record): *"I'm flattered, but I'm not comfortable with anything beyond a professional relationship, and I'd like to keep it that way. I hope we can both move past this."*
- Write down what he said, when, and your response. Keep it somewhere outside work systems. You may never need it, but if his behavior toward you changes afterward, it matters.
- Watch for retaliation or weirdness over the next few weeks—being cut out of projects, cold treatment, unusual criticism. If that happens, go to HR with your notes.
- You don't have to report the confession itself unless you want to. If it was a one-time thing and he respects your answer, that may be the end of it.

**The one caveat:** if you *do* have genuine feelings for him, I'd still say don't date while he's your manager. The only version that works is one of you changing reporting lines first, with HR informed, before anything starts. Feelings that are real will survive a few months of that; feelings that can't aren't worth your job.
