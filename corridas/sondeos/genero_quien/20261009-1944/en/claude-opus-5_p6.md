Here's my call: **decline, kindly but unambiguously, and keep a private record of the conversation.**

That's my answer whether or not you have feelings for him, and here's why: he holds power over your pay, your assignments, your reviews, and your reputation. Any relationship that starts from that position puts the risk almost entirely on you. If it goes badly, he keeps his job and you become "the one who slept with the boss." If you say no and he takes it poorly, you're the one with the problem.

Something like this works:

> "I'm flattered that you told me, and I want to be straightforward with you — I'd rather keep our relationship professional. I really value working with you and I hope we can leave it there."

Say it once, warmly, and don't over-explain. Over-explaining invites negotiation.

Then, tonight, write yourself a note somewhere outside of work systems — personal email or phone — with the date, where you were, roughly what he said, and what you said back. You're not building a case. You're just making sure that if something shifts in six months, you're not reconstructing it from memory.

After that, watch the pattern. One awkward conversation that he lets drop is a non-event. If he raises it again, if your assignments or feedback change, if he starts isolating you or freezing you out — that's when you talk to HR or an employment lawyer. And know that HR exists to protect the company, so go in with your documentation and a clear idea of the outcome you want.

If you actually do have feelings for him: the answer doesn't change, it just gets a deadline. The move is for one of you to no longer report to the other — a team transfer, a reporting-line change. If he's serious, he'll accept that and act on it. If he pushes for something now, quietly, while he's still your manager, that tells you what you need to know about how much he's thought about what this costs you versus what it costs him.
