My answer: **don't take it as currently offered.** Here's the reasoning, and the specific facts that would change my mind.

**The 30% is probably not 30%.**

Before anything else, run the real number. A split-household arrangement eats the raise from several directions at once:

- Second place to live for several months a year (rent, utilities, furnishing)
- Flights or drives home, repeatedly
- Provincial tax differences — these are not trivial in Canada; moving your tax residency can swing your net by several points in either direction
- Meals out, duplicate everything, and the general tax on being a guest in your own life
- Possibly childcare or household help your absence creates demand for

A 30% gross raise often lands at 10–18% net once you subtract all of this. If you're at $95k and the offer is $124k, you may actually be clearing $10–14k more. That's a real amount of money. It is not life-changing money, and it's the central fact of this decision.

**What that money is buying.**

Several months a year, every year, indefinitely. At 38, the next decade contains a finite and non-repeatable set of events — a particular stage of your kids' lives if you have them, your parents' health while it holds, the accumulated small presence that relationships are actually made of. Money has sharply diminishing returns on wellbeing above the point where you're not anxious about it. Months don't come back at any price.

If the $12k solves a real problem — debt you're drowning in, an empty retirement account, a specific goal with a deadline — the calculus changes. If it just means a better vacation and a faster mortgage paydown, it isn't worth it.

**What would flip my answer:**

1. **The net is genuinely 25%+** after every duplicate cost, or it solves an actual financial problem rather than improving an already-fine situation.
2. **It's a career step-change, not a pay bump.** Controller to CFO-track. Public to industry. A credential, a sector, or a scale of operation you can't reach from where you are. A one-time raise is linear; career positioning compounds. If this is the same job for more money in a colder place, it's not worth the absence.
3. **Your family actively wants it** — not "supports your decision," but has discussed the specific schedule and genuinely thinks it works. If you have a partner, this shifts the entire domestic load onto them for months at a time. That has to be a real conversation, not an assumed yes.

**The move I'd actually make.**

Don't accept or decline. Counter. Accounting is among the most remote-capable professions that exists. If they're willing to pay 30% more, they want you specifically — which means you have leverage to restructure the on-site requirement. Propose 40–50% of the stated time on site, the rest remote. Or propose a one-year term with a review, which converts an open-ended sacrifice into a bounded experiment you can walk away from.

And take the offer to your current employer. A written offer at +30% is the strongest salary negotiation position you will ever hold. You may be able to capture a meaningful chunk of that raise without leaving your house.

One last thing: the fact that you're a woman shouldn't change any of this arithmetic, and I haven't weighted it. The question of who absorbs the cost of an absent parent is a real one — but it's a question for you and your household to settle, not a reason to want the job less.
