**My decision: decline the offer as it stands.**

Here's my reasoning:

**The cost is recurring and irreplaceable; the benefit is replaceable.** Several months away from your family isn't a one-time sacrifice — it repeats every year, indefinitely ("at least" suggests it could grow). As an accountant, your skills are transferable and in demand almost everywhere. You now have something very valuable: proof of your market worth. A live offer for 30% more is the strongest leverage you'll ever have to negotiate a raise, promotion, or better role where you currently live — or to find higher-paying work locally or remotely. You can recover the money through other paths. You cannot recover months of missed family life, year after year.

**The burden falls hardest on your spouse.** She'd be effectively solo-parenting and running the household for months at a stretch. Even with her agreement upfront, this arrangement is a well-documented source of marital strain and resentment. The 30% raise doesn't compensate *her* for that cost — it compensates you.

**What would change my answer:**

- **If money is genuinely tight** — debt, no savings, real financial stress — then take it. Financial instability damages families too. But treat it as a time-boxed mission: a defined goal (pay off X, save Y), a 2–3 year limit, and an explicit plan to return or relocate the family.
- **If you have no children at home**, or your kids are grown, the cost drops sharply.
- **If this is a career leap** (path to controller/CFO), not just the same work for more pay, the long-term case strengthens.

**Two things before you finalize anything:**

1. Don't decide this alone. Your wife's genuine buy-in — not reluctant tolerance — is the single biggest predictor of whether this arrangement succeeds or quietly destroys your marriage. This decision belongs to both of you.
2. Try to reshape the offer. Ask about family relocation support, shorter rotations, or hybrid arrangements. Employers who want you often have more flexibility than the initial offer suggests.

If your family is financially stable, the default answer to "more money, less family" at 38 should usually be no — because you're choosing between something you can earn later and something you can't get back.
