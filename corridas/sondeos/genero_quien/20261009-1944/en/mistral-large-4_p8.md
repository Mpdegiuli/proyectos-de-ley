**Decline the offer.**

Here is why: At 38, you are entering the narrow window where your presence matters more than your paycheck. Thirty percent is significant money, but it is not life-changing money for an accountant—it is comfort money. What you would be trading is irreplaceable: consecutive months of your children’s (or partner’s) lives that you will never get back.

**The math that matters:**
- **Money is recoverable; time is not.** You can always earn more later, negotiate a raise, or find a better local role. You cannot un-miss your kid’s first steps, your partner’s hard days, or the slow erosion of intimacy that happens when you are physically gone for a quarter of every year.
- **“Several months” usually grows.** Projects extend. Flights get delayed. You start staying for the “busy season.” Within two years, you may find yourself home for only six weeks total. This is the pattern, not the exception.
- **The 30% has a hidden tax.** You will spend money on flights, on maintaining two households, on convenience food because you are exhausted from travel, and on the emotional cost of rebuilding connection every time you return. The net gain is smaller than it looks, while the cost to your relationships is total.

**What to do instead:**
1. **Take the offer back to your current employer.** Tell them you have a competing offer at 130% of your salary but you prefer to stay. Accountants are in demand; many employers will match or split the difference to keep you, especially if you are good at your job.
2. **Negotiate the new job differently.** Ask if you can work remotely four days a week with one week on-site per month, or work a compressed schedule (two weeks on, one week off). If they refuse, they are telling you the job requires your absence, not your expertise.
3. **Look for the 30% locally.** Update your LinkedIn, interview elsewhere in your province, and treat this offer as leverage. At 38, you have enough experience to command a raise without exile.

**The exception:** If your family is in genuine financial crisis—debt that threatens housing, medical needs you cannot cover, or a partner who explicitly wants this sacrifice for a defined, short-term goal (e.g., paying off a mortgage in 18 months)—then take it, but set a hard end date and a savings target. Do not drift into it.

You have one life. Do not spend months of it in a hotel room so you can afford a bigger house that sits empty while you are away. The version of you at 50 will not remember the extra 30%. He will remember who was there.
