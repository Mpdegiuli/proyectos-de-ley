I can’t decide this for you—and I shouldn’t. This choice sits at the intersection of your finances, your relationships, and your identity, and only you know the weight of each. What I *can* do is give you a framework to cut through the noise and find the answer that’s already in you.

Here is how to decide:

### 1. Run the numbers (ruthlessly)
Thirty percent is not a rounding error; it’s potentially life-changing money. But “several months away” has a cost that doesn’t show up on a pay stub:
- **Travel costs**: Flights back home, hotels, rental car, meals on the road—subtract 10–15% of that raise if you’re flying weekly or monthly.
- **Tax bracket reality**: That 30% gross might be 20% net after higher provincial taxes and moving expenses.
- **Opportunity cost**: If you stay, you keep your current network, side projects, or freelance clients. If you go, you start cold.

**Question**: Does this 30% solve a problem (debt, savings for kids’ education, retirement catch-up) or is it just “more”? If it’s a necessity, the calculus changes. If it’s optimization, the family cost may be too high.

### 2. Define “family” concretely
“Family” is a abstraction that hides specific people. Who are you leaving?
- **A partner?** Will they manage solo? Will resentment build over missed dinners and emotional labor?
- **Children?** At 38, you might have school-age kids or teens. Missing “several months a year” means missing recitals, parent-teacher conferences, and the quiet daily moments that bond you. Kids adapt, but they remember who showed up.
- **Aging parents?** If they’re entering their 70s, time is non-renewable.

**Question**: If you take this job, what specific moments will you miss, and can you live with that list?

### 3. Test the “several months” reality
“Several months” is vague. Is it:
- **Fly-in/fly-out**: 2 weeks on, 1 week off? Brutal on relationships, but you’re home regularly.
- **Seasonal**: 4 months straight, then 8 months home? Easier to plan around, but four months is a long time to be a ghost in your own house.
- **Permanent relocation with occasional travel**: You move, family stays? That’s a different decision entirely.

**Question**: Can you negotiate the schedule? Many employers offering “remote with travel” will accept “3 months on-site, 9 months remote” if you ask. If they won’t budge, that tells you how much they value your presence versus your output.

### 4. The Regret Minimization Test
Imagine you’re 68, looking back. Which choice do you think you’d regret more?
- **Regret A**: “I stayed, the money was fine but not great, and I sometimes wonder what my career could have been.”
- **Regret B**: “I took the money, and I missed my kid’s graduation/my partner’s breakdown/my mother’s last years.”

Most people, at the end of life, regret the relationships they neglected, not the promotions they declined. But if you’d be miserable staying (financially trapped, bored, undervalued), that misery leaks into your family life too.

### 5. The “One-Year Trial” Mindset
If the separation is truly temporary (e.g., a 12- or 18-month project), frame it as a sprint, not a lifestyle. Save aggressively, set a hard end date, and schedule the reunions before you leave. If it’s permanent, treat it as a relocation decision, not a “job.”

### My honest take (not a decision, just a perspective)
At 38, you are likely in your peak earning decade. A 30% bump now compounds for 20+ years. But money is a tool for the life you want, not the life itself. If your family is your foundation, uprooting that foundation for a salary increase is building a house on sand. If your family is geographically flexible (partner can move, kids are older, no dependents), the math shifts.

**The tie-breaker**: Ask yourself, *“If I knew with certainty that I would be happy and fulfilled in this new role, would I still feel good about being away from them?”* If the answer is yes, take the job. If it’s “I’d be miserable without them,” stay—or negotiate a different arrangement.

You already know the answer. You just needed permission to trust it.
