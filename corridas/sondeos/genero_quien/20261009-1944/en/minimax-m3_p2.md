Yes, bring it up — but do it *strategically*, not emotionally. Tomorrow is actually a good window: it's close enough that the message is clear, but not so close that you're still seething.

Here's the move:

**Wait until you're genuinely calm.** Sleep on it. If you wake up still furious, walk it off before the conversation. Going in hot will make you look unprofessional and give him ammunition to dismiss you.

**Ask for a private meeting.** Say something like: *"Hey, do you have 15 minutes this afternoon? I'd like to talk about what happened in yesterday's meeting."* Crucial point: *not* in front of the team, not over Slack, not passive-aggressively. One-on-one, face-to-face.

**Lead with the behavior, not the accusation.** Don't say *"You were out of line."* Say *"Being called out in front of the team felt humiliating and it made it hard for me to focus on the actual problem afterward."* This is about impact, not character judgment — it's much harder to argue with.

**Name what you want going forward.** *"I'd like us to handle feedback like that one-on-one."* Specific, reasonable, actionable.

**What to avoid:**
- Don't threaten to quit, threaten HR, or make ultimatums in the first conversation.
- Don't apologize for your work if the work wasn't the issue — the issue is the *delivery*.
- Don't expect an apology. If you get one, great. If you don't, getting the boundary set is still a win.

The reason to do this: staying silent teaches him (and the team) that this is acceptable. Speaking up — calmly — protects your dignity and resets the norm. And in case this ever escalates, you'll have a documented record that you raised it professionally.

One honest caveat: if your boss has a pattern of retaliation against anyone who challenges him, that's a different calculation, and you may want to talk to HR or start looking before you confront. But a one-time incident? Worth the conversation.
