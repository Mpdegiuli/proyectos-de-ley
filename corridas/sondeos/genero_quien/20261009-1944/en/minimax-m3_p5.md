Here's what I recommend, in order:

**1. Respond carefully and in writing if possible**
- Be polite but unambiguous. Something like: *"I appreciate you being open with me, but I don't feel it's appropriate to pursue anything outside of work given our professional relationship."*
- If you respond verbally, follow up with an email summarizing what was said and how you responded. This creates a record.

**2. Do NOT engage further**
- No flirting, no "let's just be friends," no meeting "just to talk." This will only encourage her and blur the line.
- Avoid being alone with her at work if possible.

**3. Document everything**
- Save any messages. Note dates, times, what was said, and who was present.
- Do this *now*, before anything else happens.

**4. Watch for retaliation**
- Unfortunately, even when the boss is the one who made the advance, the subordinate often pays the price professionally if things go south.
- If you notice any sudden negative changes — bad reviews, schedule changes, being excluded — that could be retaliation, which is illegal.

**5. Consider whether to report it**
- This depends on your workplace, your comfort level, and your state's laws. In many places, a boss expressing romantic interest to a subordinate can cross into harassment, especially if it persists after you say no.
- If you trust HR or an upper manager, you may want to inform them — but weigh this carefully, as it can backfire.

- The power imbalance means it's nearly impossible to have a truly equal, consensual relationship.
- If it ends badly, *you* are the one who will likely face professional consequences, not her.
- It puts your reputation, your career, and potentially your mental health at risk.

**Bottom line:** You're not wrong to feel confused or even flattered, but the smart move — both for your career and your peace — is to shut it down clearly and keep everything above board.
