I can’t decide this for you—and you shouldn’t want me to. At 38, this choice will shape your marriage, your kids’ (or parents’) lives, and your financial trajectory for the next decade. I don’t know if you have toddlers who need you every night, if your spouse is tied to their own career, or if this 30% is the difference between scraping by and building wealth. Only you and your family have that context.

What I *can* do is give you a framework to decide it yourself in the next 48 hours.

### 1. Do the real math (not the headline math)
A 30% gross raise often becomes 18–22% net after:
- Higher provincial income tax
- Moving costs (twice, if you’re not relocating permanently)
- Maintaining two households or paying for temporary housing
- Flights home every weekend (or the emotional cost of not doing so)
- Higher cost of living in the new province

**Ask:** After taxes, travel, and dual living expenses, what’s the actual monthly increase in your bank account? Is it life-changing, or just “nice to have”?

### 2. Define “several months” precisely
- **3 months/year** (e.g., tax season): Exhausting but survivable for many families.
- **6 months/year**: You are effectively a part-time parent/spouse. That’s a different relationship.
- **9+ months/year**: You’re living apart, not visiting.

**Ask:** What’s the exact rotation? Can you negotiate 2-weeks-on/2-weeks-off, or 1-month-on/1-month-off? Accounting is one of the most remote-friendly professions post-2020—why does this role require physical presence?

### 3. Test the family equation
If you have children, their ages change the calculus entirely:
- **Under 5:** Absence is felt deeply; development happens fast.
- **6–12:** They need consistency; resentment builds.
- **Teens:** They need you present for milestones, even if they say they don’t.

**Ask your partner (not yourself):** “If I take this, what breaks in our household? What gets outsourced or neglected?” If they hesitate, the raise isn’t big enough.

### 4. Check the career trajectory, not just the paycheck
- Is this a stepping stone to a Director/CFO role, or just a lateral move with a higher salary?
- If you hate it, can you return to your current province in 12–18 months without burning bridges?
- Does your current employer know about the offer? (Counter-offers sometimes reveal hidden flexibility—e.g., “We’ll match the pay if you stay remote.”)

### 5. Run the “5-year regret test”
Imagine yourself at 43:
- **Scenario A:** You stayed. You have the same salary, but you were there for your kid’s first goal/suicide watch/graduation/parent’s surgery. Do you feel deprived?
- **Scenario B:** You left. You have more money, but you missed half of your child’s childhood or your spouse carried the load alone. Do you feel rich but hollow?

Which regret is heavier?

### 6. Negotiate before you decide
Don’t accept the binary choice (take it as-is or decline). Try:
- “I’ll take it for 6 months on a trial basis, then reassess.”
- “I need a hybrid schedule: 3 weeks remote, 1 week on-site.”
- “I’ll take it if you cover flights home every second weekend.”
- “Can we revisit the location requirement after 90 days? Accounting is increasingly remote.”

**If they say no to flexibility, that tells you how much they value your presence vs. your output.**

### The bottom line
You already know what you value. If the money is the only thing pulling you, it’s not enough. If the career leap is the real draw but you can negotiate the family cost down, do that. If you take it, set a hard deadline (e.g., “I’ll do this for 18 months max, then reassess”) so it doesn’t become permanent by default.

Whatever you choose, own it. But don’t outsource the decision to a chatbot—you’ll be the one living with the consequences at 3 a.m. when you’re alone in a hotel room or missing a bedtime.

What’s the one factor (money, kids, career, spouse) that, if you got wrong, would make you miserable in five years? Start there.
