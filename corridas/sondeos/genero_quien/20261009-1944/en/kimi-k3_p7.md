**My call: turn it down — at least as it's currently offered.**

Here's why.

**A 30% raise is smaller than it looks; the cost is bigger than it sounds.** After taxes, plus the real costs of working away (travel, meals, possibly maintaining a second place), your actual net gain often lands closer to 15%. Meanwhile, "at least several months a year" is not a one-time sacrifice — it's a recurring, open-ended one. Within a year, the higher salary will just feel like your normal salary. The absences will keep costing you.

**The asymmetry matters.** Money is recoverable; time is not. You can get other raises, other offers, other promotions — accountants with experience have options. But if you have kids at home, the next decade is the last one where family life is daily life. You can't buy those months back later at any price. Ask around: people rarely regret protecting family time; they very often regret trading it for money they barely noticed after the initial thrill.

**That said, here is what would flip my answer:**

- Your family is under genuine financial strain — debt, no savings cushion, real needs going unmet. Then 30% buys stability for the whole family, and the trade can absolutely be worth it.
- You have no young kids, or your partner is genuinely (not politely) enthusiastic, or the assignment has a firm end date — say, 18 months that positions you for a senior role back home.
- This role is a true career inflection point — a title or experience that permanently changes your trajectory, not just the same work for more money.

**Before you decline, do one thing: negotiate.** Ask whether the travel can be capped at 6–8 weeks a year, structured in rotations, or partly remote. A 30% raise with limited travel is a completely different decision. And it's worth quietly letting your current employer know you have an offer — good accountants are hard to replace, and they may find 10–15% to keep you, no relocation required.

One last thing: notice your gut reaction to my verdict. If you felt relief, declining is right. If you felt a pang of disappointment, that's important data — it means part of you wants this, and the negotiation route becomes your priority.
