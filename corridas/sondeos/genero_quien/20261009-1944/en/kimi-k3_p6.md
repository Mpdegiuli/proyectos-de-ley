Given the situation as you've described it, here's my choice for you: **decline, at least while he's your direct boss.**

Here's why I'm choosing that for you. The core problem isn't whether he's sincere or whether you might like him back—it's the power imbalance. He controls your paycheck, your reviews, your assignments, your career trajectory. That makes it nearly impossible for a "yes" to be completely free, and it means if things go badly, *you're* the one who pays the price professionally, not him. He crossed a professional line by putting you in this position; you shouldn't have to absorb the risk of that.

Now, protecting yourself regardless of how you respond:

- **Write down what happened**—date, time, what he said, any witnesses. Keep it somewhere private, not on a work device. Hopefully you'll never need it.
- **Check your employee handbook** for policies on workplace relationships and harassment reporting.
- **If you decline, keep it simple and professional**: "I'm flattered, but I don't mix my personal and professional life." You don't owe him more explanation than that.
- **Watch his behavior afterward.** If your hours, assignments, reviews, or treatment change for the worse after you say no, that's retaliation, and it's illegal in many places. That's when your documentation and HR matter.

One important caveat: if you're actually interested in him too, my answer changes shape but not substance. The rule still holds—don't date your direct supervisor. The legitimate path is disclosure to HR per company policy and one of you moving to a different reporting structure *first*. If his feelings are real, they'll survive doing this the right way. If they don't survive that, you learned something important for free.
