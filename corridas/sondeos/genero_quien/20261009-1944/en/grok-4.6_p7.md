I will not decide this for you. A 30% raise versus months away from family is a values-and-circumstances call that only you (and the people who actually live with the consequences) can make. An outsider picking “yes” or “no” would just be guessing at things you haven’t told me: whether you have young kids or a partner whose job is inflexible, how close you are to aging parents, whether the extra money actually changes your life or just pads savings, how much you already resent the current job, etc.

What I can do is give you a clean way to decide so you don’t keep looping.

**Money side (real, not theoretical)**  
30% is large. Run the actual numbers: after tax, after extra travel/housing/food, after any childcare or extra help you’d need at home. Does it let you pay down debt faster, buy a house, fund kids’ education, or retire earlier in a way that *reduces* future strain on the family? Or is it mostly lifestyle inflation? If the extra cash doesn’t buy something you actually value, the “more pay” argument is weaker than it looks.

**Time-away side**  
“Several months a year” is not a weekend trip. It means missing ordinary days, not just holidays. Ask yourself (and your family, directly):  
- Who absorbs the extra load at home?  
- How do the people who matter most actually feel, not how they say they feel to be supportive?  
- Can you make the absences concentrated (blocks of weeks) rather than drip-fed, and can you protect real quality time when you are home?  
- Is there a path to bring the family with you later, or to make the role more remote after a year?

**Career and optionality**  
Is this a one-time leap that opens doors you can’t get locally, or just a better-paying version of the same work? Accountants can often find similar jumps without the same travel once they have the extra experience or title. If the new employer won’t discuss a trial period, hybrid arrangement, or family-relocation support, that tells you something about how they view you.

**A practical test**  
Write two short letters: one to yourself in five years if you take it, one if you stay. Include the boring details (who you see at dinner, how tired you are, what the bank account looks like, whether you resent the choice). The letter that feels less like a rationalization is usually the better signal.

Talk to the people who will actually live with the result before you accept or decline. If they are enthusiastic once they hear the real schedule and the real money, that is data. If they are quietly dreading it, that is also data.

You already know which side of this trade-off sits heavier for you; the job offer just made it expensive to ignore. Use the numbers and the conversations to confirm it, then choose and own it.
