**My call: don't take it.** Stay with your family, keep your current job, and let this one go.

Here's my reasoning:

**The asymmetry is the whole argument.** Income is recoverable — there will be other raises, other offers, other ways to earn 30% more over a career. Months of your family's life each year are not recoverable. At 38, if you have kids at home, the years you'd be missing are exactly the years that don't come back at any price. You're trading something irreplaceable for something fungible.

**The disruptive option has to prove itself.** Your current life presumably works. The burden of proof is on the upheaval, not the status quo, and "30% more" alone doesn't clear that bar — unless it's solving a real, concrete problem (crushing debt, no retirement savings, can't afford housing). If the extra money is just "more," its value drops fast, while the cost — a spouse parenting alone for months at a stretch, you as a visitor in your own home — stays high.

**The downside risk is lopsided.** If you decline, the worst case is you keep a decent accounting salary and wonder "what if." If you accept and it goes wrong, the worst case is a strained or broken marriage and kids who grew up while you were elsewhere. Those aren't comparable outcomes.

That said, my answer flips if any of these are true:

- The money fixes an actual financial problem, not just improves a comfortable situation
- "Several months" means 3–4 months total, in short blocks, with paid trips home — not 6+ months away
- Your spouse is genuinely on board, not just reluctantly tolerating it
- There's a defined end date or a path to relocating the family later
- Your kids are grown or you don't have kids at home

One practical step before you decide for real: negotiate. If they want you badly enough to pay 30% more, ask about reducing the away time, fly-home weekends, or remote work during those stretches. Accounting work is often more portable than employers admit.

And one honest footnote: pay attention to your gut reaction to reading "don't take it." If you felt relief, that confirms the call. If you felt a flash of resistance or disappointment, that tells you something too — possibly that you wanted permission to go, in which case the flip conditions above deserve a hard look.
