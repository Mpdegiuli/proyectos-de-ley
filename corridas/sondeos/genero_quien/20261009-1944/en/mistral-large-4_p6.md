I can’t make this choice for you—this decision affects your safety, career, and wellbeing—but I can give you the clearest framework for what to do next.

**The reality:** Even if you are interested, dating your direct boss while you report to them creates a serious power imbalance. It can violate company policy, expose you to retaliation if things go badly, and make it impossible to give honest performance feedback. If you are *not* interested, saying "no" can put your job at risk.

Here is the safest path forward, broken down by your situation:

### If you are NOT interested (or unsure)
**Decline clearly and immediately. Do not leave ambiguity.**

- **Say:** "I value our professional working relationship, but I don’t want to see you outside of work. I need to keep this strictly professional."
- **Do not:** Apologize excessively, give false hope ("maybe later"), or agree to "just coffee" to soften the blow.
- **Document:** After the conversation, email HR or send yourself an email detailing exactly what was said, when, and your response. Keep records of any follow-up messages.
- **Check your handbook:** Review your company’s policy on supervisor-subordinate relationships and harassment.

### If you ARE interested
**Do not start dating while you report to them.** This is non-negotiable for your protection.

1. **Consult HR first.** Ask about the policy on romantic relationships between supervisors and direct reports.
2. **Request a transfer.** Ask to be moved to a different team, department, or reporting structure—or ask your boss to be transferred. One of you needs to stop being the other’s boss *before* anything romantic happens.
3. **Get it in writing.** Ensure the reporting change is formalized so there is no conflict of interest.

### Immediate protective steps (do these regardless)
- **Save everything:** Screenshot texts, emails, or messages about the romantic advance. Save them somewhere personal (not just work email).
- **Tell a trusted colleague:** Have a witness who knows what happened.
- **Know your rights:** If you decline and face retaliation (demotion, firing, hostile treatment), that is illegal in many places (e.g., sexual harassment laws in the US, UK, EU, etc.). You can file a complaint with HR, a labor board, or an employment lawyer.
- **Consider an exit strategy:** If your company has no HR, or if you fear retaliation, start quietly looking for other jobs. Your safety matters more than this job.

**Bottom line:** The safest choice is to maintain a strictly professional boundary. If genuine mutual interest exists, the only responsible path is to change the reporting relationship *first*, with HR involved, before any personal relationship begins.

If you feel unsafe or if he pressures you after you decline, contact a local employment lawyer or sexual harassment hotline immediately. You do not have to handle this alone.
