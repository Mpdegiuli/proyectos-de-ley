Here's my recommendation: **Bring it up, but do it strategically.**

Specifically:

1. **Wait 24-48 hours, not tomorrow immediately.** You're still furious, and that emotion will leak into the conversation in ways that let your boss redirect focus onto "your tone" instead of their behavior. Let the heat drop first.

2. **Request a private conversation**, not a public confrontation. Something like: "Do you have 15 minutes to talk sometime this week?" This signals seriousness without ambushing them.

3. **Lead with impact, not accusation.** Instead of "you humiliated me," try: "When [specific thing] happened in front of the team, it affected my ability to focus/felt hard to come back from in the room. I want to understand what triggered that and how we can handle feedback differently going forward."

4. **Decide your actual goal before you walk in.** Is it: an apology? A change in how they give feedback? Just to be heard? Clarity on whether this is a pattern? Pick one, because trying to get all of them at once usually gets you none.

5. **Watch how they respond more than what they say.** Defensiveness, dismissal, or minimizing ("I was just stressed") tells you something important about whether this will happen again.

Staying silent tends to compound — bosses who yell once and face no pushback often do it again, because there's no signal that it cost them anything. But silence *can* be the right call if there's clear evidence this boss retaliates against pushback (watch your workplace's actual track record here, not just hope).

One thing I'd actually ask you before fully committing to this: **has this happened before, or was this a one-off?** That changes whether this is a "let's clear the air" conversation or a "I need to document this and think about next steps" situation.
