**Yes, bring it up — but do it the right way.** Here's my reasoning and a plan:

## Why you should address it

Letting it slide while you're this angry usually backfires. The resentment builds, your work suffers, and you signal that public humiliation is acceptable treatment. Addressing it professionally actually tends to earn respect.

## How to do it

**1. Wait until you're calm — but don't wait more than a day or two.**
Tomorrow is fine *if* you've cooled off. If you're still furious in the morning, give it one more day. You want to be composed, not seething.

**2. Request a private conversation.**
"Do you have 10 minutes today? I'd like to talk about yesterday's meeting."

**3. Keep it short, factual, and non-accusatory.** Something like:

> "I want to talk about what happened in the meeting. If I made a mistake, I'm open to hearing about it — but I'd ask that feedback like that happen privately. Being called out in front of the team made it harder for me to do my job, not easier."

**4. Then stop talking and listen.** Don't demand an apology. Don't relitigate who was right. Your goal is to set a boundary, not win a fight.

## What NOT to do

- Don't confront them while angry
- Don't do it in front of others (don't repeat their mistake)
- Don't send a long email — this needs to be face-to-face or at least a call
- Don't threaten to quit or escalate to HR as a first move

**One caveat:** If this boss has a pattern of volatility or retaliation, document the incident (date, what was said, witnesses) before the conversation, just in case.

You've got this. Calm and direct beats furious and silent every time.
