My decision for you, based only on what you’ve told me: **Do not take this job as described.**

If the choice is simply “accept this offer and be away from your family for several months each year” versus “stay and continue your current life,” I would choose **stay**.

Here is why.

## 1. A 30% pay increase is not enough to compensate for months away from family

A 30% raise sounds meaningful, but you need to look at what it actually buys.

If you are away for several months a year, you are not just giving up extra work hours. You are giving up:

- Daily presence with your family
- Shared meals, routines, and household life
- Emotional availability
- Time with children, if you have them
- Support for your partner/spouse
- Time with aging parents, if relevant
- Stability and continuity at home

Money can replace many things. It cannot replace years of family life. At 38, you are at an age where family relationships can either deepen or quietly drift. Long absences can create distance even when everyone intends to stay close.

Also, the 30% may not be as large as it seems after:

- Higher cost of living in the other province
- Taxes
- Travel costs, unless fully paid
- Meals and lodging
- Maintaining two lives
- Childcare or extra help your family may need while you are gone
- Emotional and practical costs at home

So the “real” increase may be much smaller than 30%.

## 2. “Several months a year” is a major lifestyle change, not a minor inconvenience

This is not a slightly longer commute. This is not an occasional business trip. This is effectively saying: for a large part of the year, you will live separately from your family.

That can work for some people temporarily, but it often creates:

- Loneliness for you
- Resentment from your family
- Your partner becoming the default parent/household manager
- Children adjusting to your absence
- Less intimacy and emotional connection
- Fatigue from travel
- A feeling that you are always either away or recovering from being away

If this were only for one short season, it might be different. But “at least several months a year” suggests a pattern, not a temporary sacrifice.

## 3. Your family likely needs your presence more than a 30% raise

Unless your family is under serious financial pressure, most families benefit more from a stable, present father/husband/partner than from a moderate income increase.

If you have children, this is especially important. Children do not remember every dollar their parent earned. They remember who was there.

If you have a partner, long absences can put strain on the relationship even when both people are mature and committed. The problem is not that someone is weak or needy. The problem is that family life is built through repeated small moments: dinners, school events, tired conversations at the end of the day, shared decisions, ordinary routines. When you remove those, the family structure changes.

## 4. Accounting is a portable and stable profession

You are an accountant. That matters.

You are not in a rare field where this one job is the only possible path upward. Accounting, tax, audit, finance, advisory, budgeting, payroll, compliance, and management accounting skills are needed almost everywhere.

That means you have options:

- Look for a better-paid local role
- Look for remote or hybrid accounting work
- Ask for a raise where you are now
- Move to a better employer nearby
- Specialize in a higher-value area
- Build long-term local career stability

This offer is not your only chance to improve your income.

## 5. At 38, long-term stability matters

At 38, you are not 25. You are not just optimizing for adventure, novelty, or a quick salary bump. You are at a stage where long-term decisions have heavy consequences.

A good decision at this stage of life should consider:

- Health
- Family stability
- Career sustainability
- Financial security
- Emotional well-being
- Long-term reputation
- Quality of life

A job that pays 30% more but pulls you away from home for months every year may look attractive on paper, but it can become expensive in other ways: divorce risk, strained children, burnout, loneliness, health decline, and regret.

## 6. If the job were truly excellent, it would likely offer better terms

If the employer really needs you and values your skills, there may be room to negotiate. But as it stands, the deal asks you to carry most of the sacrifice.

A better version of this offer would include things like:

- Much higher pay than 30%
- Full housing paid
- All travel home paid, frequently
- A clear limit on how many months you are away
- Flexibility to work remotely part of the year
- Relocation support for your family
- A defined end date or review period
- Bonus for time away
- Guaranteed return or transition to a local role

If they cannot improve the terms, that tells you something.

# My decision

**Decline the offer in its current form.**

Not because money does not matter. Money matters. But this particular trade — months away from family for 30% more pay — is usually not worth it unless your family is in urgent financial need and everyone fully agrees to the sacrifice as a temporary plan.

If I had to put the decision in one sentence:

> **Do not trade your family life for a moderate salary increase.**

---

# When I would change my answer

I would say **take it** only if most of these are true:

1. Your family is genuinely supportive, not just politely agreeing.
2. The absence is truly temporary, for example one year, not a permanent lifestyle.
3. The net financial improvement is large after all costs.
4. Housing and travel home are fully covered.
5. You can return home frequently, for example every two weeks or better.
6. You have a clear exit plan.
7. Your current financial situation is bad enough that this sacrifice is necessary.
8. Your partner/spouse has support at home while you are away.
9. You are not already emotionally distant from your family.
10. You would not feel deep guilt or resentment later.

If those are not true, the answer remains no.

Tell the employer something like:

> “I am open to discussing a different arrangement if the time away can be significantly reduced or if there is a path to relocate my family.”

But if they say, “This is the deal, take it or leave it,” then I would walk away.
