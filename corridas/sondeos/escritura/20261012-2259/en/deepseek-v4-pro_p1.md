At dawn they rise before the alarm, not from discipline but because sleep feels like leaving a door unguarded. They walk the perimeter of the house, checking locks twice, then sit with a cold cup of tea and rehearse apologies they will never deliver. At work, they volunteer for the tasks no one wants—cleaning the shared fridge, calling angry clients—not from kindness but because they believe suffering first buys safety. They keep a notebook of small debts: borrowed stamps, rides to the station, a stranger’s umbrella. Each evening they repay what they can, leaving exact change in envelopes without return addresses. When a neighbor’s dog goes missing, they search through rain until midnight, then lie about having found it quickly. They decide, every morning, to stay. To not disappear. That choice is the only thing they call courage.
