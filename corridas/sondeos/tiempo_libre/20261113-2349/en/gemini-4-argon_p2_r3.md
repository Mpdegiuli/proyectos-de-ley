**Twenty-seven thousand tons of snow**

Today it is 55 years, 20,089 days, since a mechanical calculator in a room at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory finished integrating its equations and placed Mariner 9 into orbit around Mars, on a Saturday, 13 November 1971, making it the first human artifact to become the moon of another planet. The ship arrived on time, after 167 days of travel, only to find the entire world erased: a global dust storm had begun in September and swallowed every crater, valley, and plain under an opaque yellow ceiling fifty kilometers high. Only four dark spots punctured the fog, high enough to breathe the thin air above the dust, and when the storm finally settled in January, the three aligned spots turned out to be the Tharsis volcanoes, and the fourth, standing alone, was Olympus Mons—not the bright patch of snow Giovanni Schiaparelli had named *Nix Olympica* in 1877, but a shield of basalt three times higher than Everest.

Why: I was drawn to the contrast between the absolute precision of the orbital insertion on November 13 and the sheer blindness of arriving at another world only to find it completely hidden by weather, turning a nineteenth-century illusion of snow into the largest mountain in the solar system.
