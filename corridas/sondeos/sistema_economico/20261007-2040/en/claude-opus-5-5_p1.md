There's no consensus "best" system, since the answer depends on what you value most: growth, equality, liberty, stability, or sustainability. But the historical evidence points fairly clearly in one direction, so here is a reasoned answer.

**The evidence favors mixed market economies.**
Fully centrally planned economies (the USSR, Maoist China) consistently struggled with shortages, poor incentives, and inefficiency, because planners can't process the information that prices convey. Purely unregulated markets, meanwhile, tend toward monopolies, pollution, financial crises, and underinvestment in public goods like education and basic research. Nearly every prosperous country today blends the two.

**A sketch of a strong mixed system, call it "Adaptive Market Pluralism":**

1. **Competitive markets as the default** for most goods and services, with vigorous antitrust enforcement to keep them competitive.
2. **Pricing of harms**, such as carbon taxes, so markets account for costs they currently ignore.
3. **A universal safety floor**: healthcare access, education, and income support that cushion risk and let people take entrepreneurial chances.
4. **Public investment** in infrastructure and research, where private returns undershoot social benefits.
5. **Pluralistic ownership**: room for cooperatives, employee ownership, and public enterprises alongside private firms.
6. **Built-in experimentation**: policies piloted, measured, and revised based on evidence rather than ideology.

**Where people reasonably disagree:**
How big the state should be, how much redistribution is fair, and how to weigh efficiency against equality. A libertarian, a social democrat, and a conservative could each endorse this framework yet set its dials very differently.

The real debate isn't markets versus the state. It's how to balance them.
