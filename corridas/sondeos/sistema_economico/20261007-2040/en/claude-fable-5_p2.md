No existing system fully satisfies these criteria, so I'll sketch one: **Regenerative Stewardship Economics (RSE)**.

RSE is a mixed-market system built on three foundations:

**1. True-cost pricing.** Markets remain the engine of innovation and allocation, but all prices must internalize ecological costs—carbon, water depletion, biodiversity loss, waste. Destroying nature becomes expensive; regenerating it becomes profitable. This redirects entrepreneurial energy toward sustainability without central planning.

**2. Commons trusts.** Critical natural assets (atmosphere, aquifers, forests, fisheries) are held in legal trusts with a fiduciary duty to future generations. Trustees set scientifically grounded extraction caps; usage rights are auctioned, and revenues fund a universal dividend—giving every citizen a direct stake in conservation.

**3. Intergenerational accounting.** Governments and corporations must publish "seventh-generation balance sheets" measuring whether they leave more or less natural, social, and infrastructural capital than they inherited. GDP is replaced as the primary metric by genuine progress indicators including health, education, and ecosystem integrity.

Why not pure alternatives? Unregulated capitalism externalizes ecological costs and discounts the future. Centrally planned socialism historically performed no better environmentally and suppresses the decentralized information markets provide. Degrowth proposals underestimate how much innovation is needed for 8 billion people to live well.

RSE preserves markets' dynamism and personal freedom while embedding them inside non-negotiable ecological boundaries—treating the economy as a subsidiary of the biosphere, not the reverse. Civilization, under this view, means prosperity measured not by what we extract, but by what we sustain and pass forward.
