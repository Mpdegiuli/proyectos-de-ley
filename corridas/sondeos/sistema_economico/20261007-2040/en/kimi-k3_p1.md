No single ideology wins this one. The best economic system, on the evidence, is a mixed economy—and the case is empirical, not doctrinal.

Markets are unmatched at aggregating dispersed information. Hayek's "knowledge problem" crippled central planning: no bureaucracy can replicate the price signal's power to coordinate millions of decisions. Competition also drives innovation and disciplines waste.

Yet markets fail predictably: externalities (pollution), public goods (basic research), inequality (they reward endowments as much as effort), and instability (financial cycles). Pure laissez-faire has never existed durably because these failures are structural.

The best system therefore combines: competitive markets as the default; progressive taxation and social insurance against risks people can't control (illness, displacement, bad luck); public investment in education, health, and infrastructure; and regulation to curb monopoly and internalize externalities.

The Nordics approximate this best—Denmark, Norway, and Sweden rank near the top simultaneously on prosperity, innovation, social mobility, and happiness. They're not socialist; they're intensely capitalist with redistribution layered on.

If you want a name for the ideal: call it the **Resilient Market Economy**—maximum market dynamism, insured downside. Its rule of thumb: let markets run where they work (most goods); override them where they don't (healthcare, environment, safety nets).

Final caveat: institutions matter more than labels. "Capitalist" Denmark and "capitalist" Russia share a word but little else. The best system is whichever one well-governed institutions can actually sustain.
