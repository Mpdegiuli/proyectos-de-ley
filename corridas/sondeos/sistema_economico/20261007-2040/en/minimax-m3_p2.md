**The Regenerative Commons Economy** — a synthesis drawing from ecological economics, social democracy, and commons theory.

**Core mechanisms:**

1. **Ecological ceilings, social foundations.** Modeled on Kate Raworth's doughnut: the economy operates between a minimum quality-of-life floor (healthcare, housing, food, clean water, education) and a maximum ecological ceiling (carbon budgets, biodiversity thresholds, extraction caps). Boundaries are set democratically and revised with scientific input.

2. **Mixed ownership, strong commons.** Water, energy grids, healthcare, transit, and digital infrastructure are held as public commons or social cooperatives. Consumer goods flow through regulated markets.

3. **Fully internalized externalities.** Carbon taxes, resource extraction caps, pollution permits, and strict liability laws ensure prices reflect true ecological and social costs.

4. **Democratic governance.** Participatory budgeting shapes investment priorities; co-determination rights extend to workers; corporations have stakeholder, not only shareholder, duties.

5. **Intergenerational institutions.** A constitutional Future Generations Commissioner—veto power over legislation undermining long-term wellbeing, modeled on Wales—guards tomorrow's interests today.

6. **Beyond GDP.** Wellbeing, resilience, and ecosystem health replace growth as primary metrics. Wealthy nations embrace sufficiency; developing nations pursue sustainable prosperity.

**Why this works:** Civilization advances not through accumulation but through cultivated capabilities, relationships, and resilience. Markets remain useful servants but never become ends in themselves. By capping throughput while guaranteeing a dignified floor, the system reconciles human flourishing with planetary boundaries and intergenerational justice—answering all three requirements at once.
