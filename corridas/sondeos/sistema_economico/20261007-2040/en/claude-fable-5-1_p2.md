No existing system fully fits, so I'd propose a hybrid I'll call **Stewardship Market Economy**.

**Core idea:** Keep markets for what they do well—allocating goods, rewarding innovation, coordinating millions of decisions—but embed them inside hard ecological and social boundaries set democratically, not left to price signals alone.

**Key features:**

1. **True-cost pricing.** Carbon, pollution, water depletion, and biodiversity loss are taxed or capped so prices reflect real costs. Revenue is returned as a citizen dividend, protecting the poor.

2. **Commons trusts.** Forests, fisheries, atmosphere, and aquifers are held in legal trusts with a fiduciary duty to future generations, insulated from short-term politics and corporate capture.

3. **Universal foundations.** Healthcare, education, and basic housing are guaranteed—because people worried about survival cannot think long-term about nature or anything else.

4. **Circular rules.** Products must be repairable and recyclable by design; extraction is capped at regeneration rates.

5. **Wealth recycling.** Progressive inheritance and land-value taxes prevent dynastic concentration, which historically corrodes both democracy and ecological restraint.

6. **Long-term governance.** A "future generations" ombudsman with legal standing, plus 20-year budgeting horizons.

**Why not alternatives?** Pure capitalism discounts the future and externalizes costs. Central planning lacks information and stifles the creativity needed for green technology. Degrowth alone offers no path for poor nations.

The genius of this model is modesty: it doesn't bet on human virtue. It aligns incentives so that doing the sustainable thing is also the profitable, convenient thing—turning self-interest into stewardship.
