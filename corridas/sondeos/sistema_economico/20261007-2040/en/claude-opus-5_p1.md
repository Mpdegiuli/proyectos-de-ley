**The caveat:** this is partly a values question, so what follows is a judgment, not a finding.

**My pick:** a competitive market economy with socialized risk — roughly what the Nordic countries approximate, though I'd modify the tax base.

**Why:**

Markets solve a problem central planning has never solved: aggregating dispersed information about what people want and what things actually cost. The 20th century ran this experiment with unusual cleanliness — divided Germany, divided Korea — and the verdict was lopsided.

But markets fail *predictably*, not marginally: pollution and other externalities, public goods, monopoly, information asymmetry in health care and finance, and the fact that what you earn reflects bargaining power at least as much as contribution. Birth lottery outcomes aren't deserved in any morally interesting sense.

So: keep private firms and price signals, but tax economic rents hardest — land values, pollution, inheritance, monopoly profits — since these are the taxes that distort production least. Then fund health care, education, childcare, and income insurance universally, so that risk is borne collectively rather than by whoever happens to get unlucky. People take more entrepreneurial risk when failure isn't catastrophic.

The case is partly empirical: this arrangement produces high incomes, long lives, high reported wellbeing, and strong civil liberties simultaneously.

**What I'm unsure about:** whether it works without pre-existing state capacity and high trust; whether it can handle ecological limits; and whether concentrated wealth eventually captures the regulators it depends on. That last one worries me most.

Confidence: moderate, not high.
