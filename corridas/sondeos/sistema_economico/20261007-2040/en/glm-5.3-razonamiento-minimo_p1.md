There's no single "best" economic system—context matters—but if I had to pick, I'd defend a **regulated market economy with a strong social safety net** (the Nordic-influenced model, sometimes called "social capitalism").

**Why markets?** No system yet devised matches markets at aggregating dispersed information and coordinating millions of decisions. Central planning failed not mainly because planners were foolish, but because the information problem is intractable: prices encode knowledge no committee can gather. Markets also drive innovation and efficiency through competition and profit incentives.

**Why regulation?** Markets have systematic failures: monopolies, externalities like pollution, short-termism, and instability (as 2008 showed). Unregulated capitalism also tends toward extreme inequality, which corrodes both democracy and the meritocratic ideals markets need to function.

**Why a safety net?** Beyond the moral argument that no one should starve in a wealthy society, there's a practical one: security makes people *more* willing to take entrepreneurial risks. Denmark's "flexicurity" shows you can have dynamic labor markets plus generous protections.

**Honest limitations:** This model isn't perfect. It can breed complacency, struggle with slow-moving crises like climate change, and depends on strong institutions that are hard to build and easy to erode.

The deeper answer is that "best" is really "best adaptation to a country's history, institutions, and stage of development." The same policies that work in Denmark can fail where institutions are weak. The best economic system, ultimately, is one that stays adaptable—preserving markets' dynamism while continuously correcting their failures, rather than treating any ideology as fixed and final.
