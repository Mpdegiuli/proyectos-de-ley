I'd pick something close to the Nordic/social-market model, which I'll call **market-anchored social democracy** since no single country fully embodies it.

**Core design:**
- **Markets allocate** goods, labor, and capital. Decentralized prices aggregate information no planner can match, and competition drives innovation. The 20th century's natural experiments (East/West Germany, North/South Korea) settled this.
- **The state handles what markets botch**: it taxes pollution and other externalities, breaks up monopolies, provides universal health care and education, and runs a robust safety net financed by broad, efficient taxes (VAT, land, carbon) rather than heavy capital taxes that deter investment.
- **Flexicurity**: easy hiring and firing, paired with generous unemployment support and retraining. Protect people, not jobs.
- **Strong institutions**: rule of law, independent courts, low corruption, transparent regulation.

**Why this and not alternatives?**
Pure laissez-faire ignores externalities, information asymmetries, and the fact that extreme inequality corrodes both democracy and opportunity. Central planning fails on information and incentives. This hybrid has the best empirical record: Denmark, Norway, Finland, and Sweden combine high GDP per capita, high innovation, high social mobility, and the world's highest reported wellbeing.

**Honest caveat:** It depends on trust and state capacity, which are hard to build and easy to lose. It works less well in large, heterogeneous, or corrupt polities. So the real answer is: markets plus a competent, accountable state, calibrated to local institutions—and the calibration is where all the hard work lies.
