If I had to choose, I'd pick what might be called an **adaptive mixed economy** — essentially the social market model (think Denmark, Germany, or the Nordic countries), but with an explicit commitment to revising itself as evidence accumulates.

**Why markets at the core:** Markets solve an otherwise intractable problem — coordinating billions of decisions about what to produce and how, using decentralized knowledge no planner can access. Price signals and competition drive innovation and efficiency better than any alternative humanity has tried. The failures of centrally planned economies weren't accidents of implementation; they reflected a fundamental information problem.

**Why not pure laissez-faire:** Markets predictably fail in known ways — externalities (pollution), public goods (basic research, infrastructure), monopoly power, information asymmetries, and distributions of wealth that can undermine both fairness and the political stability markets depend on. A system that ignores these failures erodes its own foundations.

**So the design is:** competitive markets for most goods and services; strong public provision or regulation where market failures are severe (healthcare, education, environment); robust safety nets that protect people rather than specific jobs, preserving both security and economic dynamism; and antitrust enforcement to keep markets actually competitive.

**The "adaptive" part matters most.** No system is optimal across all technologies and eras. Automation, AI, and climate change may demand tools we haven't needed before. The best system treats its own rules as provisional — judged by outcomes like broad prosperity, mobility, and human flourishing, not ideology.

It's unglamorous, but it has the best track record.
